# Lilith in the Alphabet of Ben Sira — Medieval Folklore Source Packet

## Editorial judgment

This is a deliberately separate medieval card, not evidence for a missing woman in Genesis. The *Alphabet of Ben Sira* is an anonymous, frequently comic and transgressive work composed in the early medieval period, commonly dated between the eighth and tenth centuries. Its Lilith episode explains a protective birth amulet. The card must narrate what this particular text says without converting it into either biblical history or a modern emancipation manifesto.

## Controlling narrative

In the alternative version of the *Alphabet of Ben Sira*:

- When Adam is alone, God creates a woman from earth as Adam was created and calls her Lilith.
- Adam and Lilith quarrel over who will lie beneath the other. Adam claims superiority; Lilith answers that they are equal because both were made from earth.
- Unable to agree, Lilith pronounces the Ineffable Name and flies into the air. The text does **not** say that she was “given the Name alongside Adam.”
- Adam prays that the woman given him has fled. God sends the three healing angels Sanoy, Sansanoy, and Semangelof to retrieve her.
- They find her in the sea where Egyptians are later destined to drown. She refuses to return, identifying her purpose with harming infants.
- The angels threaten that one hundred of her children will die each day. She swears by God's name that when she sees those angels, their names, or their forms on an amulet, she will not harm the protected infant. The tale is presented to explain the amulet Ben Sira makes for Nebuchadnezzar's sick child.

Primary Hebrew witness: *Otzar Midrashim*, Alphabet of Ben Sira, alternative version 34. A checked bilingual presentation is available in Sefaria's source library. Local source history: `docs/scribe-texts/jud/ohpe/index.md` distinguishes the proverbial Alphabet from the later Midrash; `docs/scribe-texts/jud/jms/jms09.md` and `jms24.md` document the three amulet names and the medieval textual layer.

## Boundaries and corrections

- Genesis does not name Lilith as Adam's first wife, and “Genesis 1–3 implied” should not appear as biblical evidence. The medieval author reworks Genesis 2:18; that is reception, not implication.
- “The First Who Refused” is a modern epithet, not a translation of לִילִית.
- The story does not call Lilith queen of night demons, say she rules the lilim, or frame her choice in the abstract vocabulary of “covenant.”
- Her equality argument is directly attested; so are the lethal claims about infants and her own children. Neither half should be erased to make her solely heroine or solely monster.
- The work's satirical/folkloric setting must remain explicit. Crazy Alchemist is discovery only and does not control the card.

## Artwork verdict

Replace the seductive demon-queen portrait with the text's confrontation at the sea: Lilith stands over dark wind-torn water, still humanlike because the tale gives no monstrous anatomy, refusing three plainly robed messenger-angels who face her together. One angel holds a small protective amulet bearing non-legible ancient marks; distant violent water anticipates the later Egyptian drowning. Her raised hand communicates refusal, while the angels' grave posture communicates the threat and negotiated oath.

Required exclusions: serpent body, horns, bat wings, red skin, nude glamour pose, crown, demon throne, Eden scene, Adam in the same frame, feminist poster symbolism, infant attack, or legible fabricated divine names.
