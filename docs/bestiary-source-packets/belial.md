# Belial — Hebrew Idiom and Qumran Commander Source Packet

## Editorial judgment

The name has a textual history, not one unchanged biography. In much of the Hebrew Bible, בְּלִיַּעַל marks worthlessness, lawlessness, ruin, or wicked persons—especially in the idiom “sons/men of Belial.” In Second Temple texts, and with exceptional clarity at Qumran, Belial is personalized as the ruler of a hostile lot and army. The War Scroll controls the artwork; later grimoire kingship and angel-chariot anatomy are excluded.

## Hebrew Bible layer

- Deuteronomy 13:13, Judges 19:22 and 20:13, 1 Samuel 2:12, 10:27, 25:17/25, 30:22, and other passages use “sons/men of Belial” for wicked, base, or lawless people rather than children of a named demon.
- Deuteronomy 15:9 warns against a “word/thought of Belial” in the heart; Psalms 18:4 and 41:8 use the term in ruinous or deathly imagery.
- The etymology remains debated. “Worthlessness” is a responsible conventional gloss; “without yoke” is an old interpretation, not settled morphology.

## Qumran layer

- **War Scroll 1QM I:** the Sons of Light go out against the Sons of Darkness, described as the army/host of Belial. The conflict proceeds through lots until God's decisive victory and Belial's overthrow.
- **1QM XIII:** the liturgy blesses the God of Israel and curses Belial and the spirits of his lot. God appointed the Prince of Light to aid the righteous; Belial is an angel of hostility/malevolence whose dominion is darkness, and the spirits of his lot bring wickedness.
- **1QM XIV–XVII:** Belial's rule and assembled forces press the battle, but the covenant community praises God for preserving the remnant and sending Michael's authority to exalt the lot of light.
- **Community Rule 1QS III–IV:** the Prince of Lights and Angel of Darkness govern opposed lots; the latter causes even sons of righteousness to stray during the appointed period. Related Qumran passages name Belial as the hostile ruler.
- **11QMelchizedek:** Belial and spirits of his lot stand opposed in the eschatological deliverance/judgment scene.

The texts subordinate both lots to God's times and judgment. Belial is formidable but neither equal to God nor an independent cosmic principle.

## Later boundaries

Second Corinthians 6:15 places Beliar/Belial opposite Christ, showing the personalized adversarial use. Testaments and Ascension of Isaiah traditions develop a prince of lawlessness. The much later *Lesser Key of Solomon* makes Belial a mighty king appearing as two beautiful angels in a fiery chariot; that grimoire image is not Qumran evidence and is barred from this card.

## Artwork verdict

Show the War Scroll's two-level battlefield: ordered Sons of Light in historically grounded Second Temple-era formation under pale banners and priestly trumpets; across the valley, a dense human army of darkness advances beneath blackened standards. Above and behind the latter host, Belial is only an immense, anatomically unresolved angelic shadow within storm and smoke, surrounded by lesser shadow-spirits of his lot. A restrained shaft of divine dawn breaks toward the light camp. This depicts authority and lot without inventing a demon-prince portrait.

Required exclusions: throne, crown, fiery chariot, two beautiful angels, goat head, horns, red skin, bat wings, grimoire seal, medieval armor, modern occult symbols, readable or pseudo-Hebrew, or equal-sized good and evil gods. Shared Ancient Witness encaustic/egg-tempera style.
