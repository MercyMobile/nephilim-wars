Xerxes (Ahasuerus) Royal Inscriptions

Trilingual royal inscriptions of Xerxes I · 486-465 BCE

Overview

Xerxes I (Old Persian Xšayāršā), son of Darius I, ruled the Persian empire 486-465 BCE. His royal inscriptions are carved in three languages: Old Persian, Elamite and Babylonian. They follow a set formula: praise of Ahura Mazda 'who created this earth, who created yonder heaven, who created man, who created welfare for man, who made Xerxes king', then Xerxes' titles and descent from Darius, then a statement of building and a prayer for protection.

The Gate of All Nations text (XPa), repeated four times above the winged bulls of the Persepolis gateway, says Xerxes built 'this colonnade (for the representatives) of all countries'. The Van rock inscription (XV) says Darius had the niche cut but left it uninscribed, and Xerxes ordered the text written. The 'Daiva inscription' (XPh), found on stone tablets at Persepolis in June 1935, records a cultic reform: it condemns the worship of the daivas (rival gods) and enforces the worship of Ahura Mazda (as summarized by Barnea 2025; the full XPh text is not cited here from a public-domain translation).

None of these inscriptions mentions Esther, Mordecai, Haman, Jews or Judah. The biblical link is the king's name: Hebrew 'Ahasuerus' (ʾAḥašwērôš) is a Hebrew rendering of Persian Xšayāršā, whose Aramaic form appears in the Elephantine papyri. The inscriptions show the court titles, the scale of the empire and the religious ideology of the king whom Esther and Ezra 4:6 call Ahasuerus.

In its own words

Says Xerxes the great king: By the grace of Ahura Mazda, this colonnade (for the representatives) of all countries I made; much else (that is) beautiful (was) done throughout Persia which I did and which my father did.H. C. Tolman, Ancient Persian Lexicon and the Texts of the Achaemenidan Inscriptions (1908), 'Xerx. Pers. a', §3, p. 39

What the evidence shows

  • XPa: 'Four times repeated on propylaea of Xerxes above the sculptured winged bulls; Persian, Elamite, Babylonian' (Tolman 1908, p. 39).
  • Standard titulary: 'I (am) Xerxes the great king, king of kings, king of the countries possessing many kinds of people, king of this great earth far and wide, the son of Darius the king, the Achaemenide' (Tolman 1908).
  • XV (Van): Darius 'commanded to dig out this place where he did not make an inscription written; afterwards I commanded to write this inscription' (Tolman 1908).
  • XPh: found at Persepolis 26 June 1935 on stone tablets, two with the Old Persian text, one Akkadian, one Elamite; first full publication of the original texts by E. Herzfeld, Archäologische Mitteilungen aus Iran 8 (1936) 56-77 (Kent 1937).
  • Elephantine: an Aramaic contract is dated 'the 28th of the month Paophi in the 2nd year of King Xerxes' (Cowley no. 2, 484 BCE); Cowley notes the Aramaic spellings of the name against 'OP Khshayarsha'.
  • Elephantine Cowley no. 6 equates year 21 of Xerxes with year 1 of Artaxerxes I (465 BCE), fixing the end of the reign.

Open questions

  • Is the Esther story's Ahasuerus the historical Xerxes? The name equation rests on the consonants of Hebrew ʾḤŠWRŠ and Persian Xšayāršā / Aramaic forms of the same name in Elephantine papyri; Ezra 4:6 places an Ahasuerus between Darius and Artaxerxes, the order of Darius I, Xerxes I, Artaxerxes I. S. Dalley (Esther's Revenge at Susa, OUP 2007) argues from Assyrian words, phrases and customs in the book that the story's roots go back to 7th-century Assyria and Susa. The Persian inscriptions themselves are silent on any queen named Esther or Vashti.
  • What did the Daiva inscription reform? J. Degen (JNES 83/1, 2024) reads XPh against Assyrian royal models ('an heir of Assyria?'). G. Barnea (Iran and the Caucasus 29/2, 2025) centres the reform on the repeated phrase artācā brzmniy and offers a wider translation using onomastic, epigraphic and archaeological data. Which country or sanctuary the reform targeted is not settled by the texts read here; identifications proposed in the literature (Babylon, Egypt, elsewhere) are inference.
  • Did Xerxes rebuild or destroy temples? The Daiva inscription condemns daiva worship (Barnea 2025) while the Gate text claims building. Neither mentions Jerusalem. Any link to the accusation against Judah and Jerusalem 'in the beginning of his reign' (Ezra 4:6) is an inference, not something the inscriptions state.

The Find

  • DateReign of Xerxes I, 486-465 BCE. Individual texts are undated; they are placed in the reign by the king's name and titulary ('Xerxes the great king... son of Darius the king, the Achaemenid')
  • How datedName and filiation in the texts; the Elephantine papyri independently date documents by Xerxes' regnal years (Cowley no. 2: year 2 of Xerxes = 484 BCE) and equate year 21 of Xerxes with year 1 of Artaxerxes I (465 BCE; Cowley no. 6).
  • DiscoveredXPa and other Persepolis texts known to European travellers since the 17th-19th centuries; XPh found 26 June 1935, XPh: Oriental Institute (University of Chicago) Persepolis expedition (E. Herzfeld; E. F. Schmidt), Persepolis (Gate of All Nations, palaces, stone tablets of XPh); Mount Elvend near Hamadan (XE); the citadel rock at Van, eastern Turkey (XV)
  • Now heldXPa, XE and XV are carved in situ (Persepolis, Ganjnameh near Hamadan, Van citadel). The XPh stone tablets (two Old Persian, one Akkadian, one Elamite) were found at Persepolis in 1935; their present museum location was Not confirmed in a published museum record.
  • MaterialCarved stone (building and rock-face inscriptions); XPh on stone tablets shaped like clay tablets
  • ScriptOld Persian cuneiform, Elamite cuneiform, Babylonian cuneiform (trilingual)
  • LanguageOld Persian, Elamite, Akkadian (Babylonian)

Biblical Passages

  • Ezra 4:6'In the reign of Ahasuerus, in the beginning of his reign, wrote they unto him an accusation against the inhabitants of Judah and Jerusalem.' The verse sits between Darius (4:5) and Artaxerxes (4:7), the same order as Darius I - Xerxes I - Artaxerxes I in the Persian records and the Elephantine date formulas.
  • Esth 1:1-3; 10:1Ahasuerus reigns over many provinces from Susa and imposes tribute on land and isles. Xerxes' titulary 'king of kings, king of the countries possessing many kinds of people, king of this great earth far and wide' is the Persian self-description of the same scale of rule. The inscriptions do not mention Susa's court events or any person in Esther.
  • Dan 11:2'The fourth shall be far richer than they all ... he shall stir up all against the realm of Grecia': a Persian king rich and campaigning against Greece fits Xerxes' place in the royal sequence. The Xerxes inscriptions do not narrate the Greek campaign; this is our reading of the verse, set beside the king's own record.
