Nehemiah's Wall

Claimed remains of the wall Nehemiah rebuilt · 5th century BCE (disputed)

Overview

The book of Nehemiah says that Nehemiah, cupbearer to King Artaxerxes, came to Jerusalem as governor, found its walls broken down and gates burnt, and organized the repair of the whole circuit in 52 days (Neh 6:15), with work gangs assigned to named sections and gates (Neh 3). No inscription naming Nehemiah has been found in any wall.

On the crest of the eastern slope of the City of David stand a stretch of city wall and two towers, excavated in turn by Macalister and Duncan in the 1920s, Kenyon in the 1960s and Shiloh in the 1970s-80s. Kenyon identified a segment of wall north of the northern tower with Nehemiah's fortification; Kenyon and Shiloh dated the Southern Tower to the Hasmonean period, while Macalister and Duncan had called it post-exilic, 5th-4th century BCE (as recorded in Mazar's 2009 endnotes). Eilat Mazar, re-excavating the area from 2005, later argued that the Northern Tower belonged to the Persian period and so to Nehemiah (BAR, March/April 2009, 'The Wall That Nehemiah Built'; the article's evidence section is paywalled and was not read here).

Finkelstein, Herzog, Singer-Avitz and Ussishkin (Tel Aviv 34, 2007) note that Mazar's own 2007 report had assigned the eastern city wall and both towers to the late Hellenistic Hasmonean fortification and that 'no remains were assigned to the Persian period' in the Large Stone Structure. Finkelstein (JSOT 32, 2008) argued the Persian-period settlement was small and that the archaeology, read without the Nehemiah account as a guide, does not show a Persian-period wall. The question is open: the ground shows a wall line reused over centuries; the text shows a rebuilding; no find yet links the two directly.

What the evidence shows

  • A city-wall segment and two towers stand on the crest of the City of David's eastern slope, above the Stepped Stone Structure (Finkelstein et al. 2007, describing Mazar 2007).
  • Kenyon (1974: 191) identified the wall segment north of the northern tower with Nehemiah's fortifications (as cited in Finkelstein et al. 2007).
  • Mazar's 2007 preliminary report dated the eastern city wall and its two towers to the late Hellenistic (Hasmonean) fortification (as summarized in Finkelstein et al. 2007).
  • In BAR 35/2 (March/April 2009) Mazar argued for a Nehemiah-period date; the article's endnotes record that Macalister and Duncan dated the Southern Tower to the 'Post-Exilic period (fifth-fourth century B.C.E.)' while Kenyon and Shiloh dated it Hasmonean.
  • Renewed Giv'ati Parking Lot excavations found late Iron Age, Persian and Early Hellenistic remains on the western slope; after 586 BCE the city was rebuilt along the summit and north-western slope (Shalev, Shalom, Bocher and Gadot, Tel Aviv 47, 2020).
  • The mid-slope fortification and retaining wall on the eastern slope were radiocarbon-dated to after the earthquake of c. 760 BCE, reign of Uzziah, not Hezekiah (Regev et al., PNAS 121, 2024). This fixes the date of an Iron Age wall line that a 5th-century rebuilding could have followed or reused; it does not date any Persian-period work.

Open questions

  • Is the Northern Tower Persian or Hasmonean? E. Mazar (BAR, March/April 2009) dates it to Nehemiah's time; the evidence she gives there sits behind the paywall and has not been reviewed for this entry. Finkelstein, Herzog, Singer-Avitz and Ussishkin (Tel Aviv 34/2, 2007) cite Mazar's own earlier report assigning the city wall and towers to the Hasmonean fortification and argue that most elements in this area are late Hellenistic, with 'circumstantial evidence' for that date. Pottery under a structure gives only the earliest possible date; neither side has published a sealed foundation deposit for the tower in the sources seen here.
  • Was Persian-period Jerusalem big enough to need a circuit wall? Finkelstein (JSOT 32/4, 2008) argues activity in the Persian and early Hellenistic City of David was sparse and concentrated south of Mazar's area; the same study opens his book Hasmonean Realities behind Ezra, Nehemiah, and Chronicles, whose title states his view that these books reflect later, Hasmonean-period conditions. Shalev, Shalom, Bocher and Gadot (Tel Aviv 47, 2020) show Persian and Early Hellenistic finds on the western slope and the summit. O. Lipschits' 2024 collected essays include 'Persian Period Finds from Jerusalem: Facts and Interpretations' and a reassessment of the city's Persian-period development. The size of the settlement is still argued from sherds and fills, not from a Persian-period wall line.
  • Repair or new build? Nehemiah 3 describes repairing existing walls and gates section by section. If Nehemiah followed the Iron Age line, the job was repair of standing ruins, which fits the 52-day figure better than new construction on a new line. That is our reading of the text; the archaeology has not identified which line he used.

  • 2024-03O. Lipschits' collected essays (De Gruyter) republished his assessments of Persian-period finds from Jerusalem and of the city's development in that period. [6]

The Find

  • DateDisputed. Nehemiah's rebuilding is set by the text in the 20th year of Artaxerxes (Neh 2:1), i.e. c. 445 BCE if Artaxerxes I. The masonry proposed as his wall (the 'Northern Tower' and adjoining wall on the crest of the City of David's eastern slope) has been dated by excavators and critics to the Persian period (E. Mazar 2009), to the late Hellenistic / Hasmonean period (Kenyon for the towers; Finkelstein et al. 2007, summarizing Mazar's own 2007 report), or left open.
  • How datedPottery from fills under and against the tower and wall; no inscription or datable foundation deposit ties any wall segment to Nehemiah. The Artaxerxes identification is fixed by Elephantine: Cowley 30 shows Bigvai as governor of Judah in 408 BCE, so Nehemiah's 32nd year of Artaxerxes (Neh 13:6) must be Artaxerxes I (Cowley 1923).
  • DiscoveredWall line exposed by R. A. S. Macalister and J. G. Duncan (1920s) and K. Kenyon (1960s); re-excavated 2005-2008 by Eilat Mazar, who later argued for a Nehemiah date for the Northern Tower (BAR 2009), Macalister and Duncan; Kathleen Kenyon; Yigal Shiloh; Eilat Mazar, Crest of the eastern slope of the City of David (south-eastern ridge), Jerusalem, above the Stepped Stone Structure
  • Now heldIn situ, City of David, Jerusalem
  • MaterialDressed and rough stone masonry (tower and city-wall segment); no mudbrick is reported in the sources checked

Biblical Passages

  • Neh 1:3; 2:13-17Report that 'the wall of Jerusalem also is broken down, and the gates thereof are burned'; Nehemiah's night inspection. Destruction of 586 BCE is attested in Jerusalem's burnt layers; which walls were still standing in 445 BCE is not known from excavation.
  • Neh 3Section-by-section list of repairs and gates (Sheep Gate, Fish Gate, Fountain Gate, 'the wall of the pool of Siloah by the king's garden' 3:15). Mazar used these toponyms to identify buildings; Finkelstein et al. (2007) object to using them for earlier periods. No gate named in Neh 3 has been identified by inscription.
  • Neh 6:15'So the wall was finished ... in fifty and two days.' A 52-day job implies repair of an existing line, not new foundations; the text itself uses 'repaired' throughout ch. 3.
  • Neh 2:10, 19; 13:6Sanballat the Horonite opposes the work; Elephantine papyrus Cowley 30 (408 BCE) names 'Delaiah and Shelemiah the sons of Sanballat governor of Samaria', which with Bigvai's governorship fixes Nehemiah under Artaxerxes I.
