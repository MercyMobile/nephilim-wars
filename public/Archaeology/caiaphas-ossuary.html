Caiaphas Ossuary

Inscribed limestone bone box from a family tomb in Jerusalem · 1st century CE

Overview

In late November 1990 workmen landscaping a water park in Jerusalem's Peace Forest broke into a rock-cut burial cave with four loculi (kokhim). Zvi Greenhut of the Israel Antiquities Authority excavated it. The cave held twelve ossuaries; four had been pulled out by the workmen, two lay in situ in loculus IV and six were scattered in the chamber. Five ossuaries carried names.

Two ossuaries carry a form of the family name Qafa' / Qayafa', the first time the name had turned up in an archaeological context. One is inscribed simply 'Qafa''. The other, the most elaborately decorated box in the cave (two circles, each holding a central rosette ringed by five whorl rosettes), found undisturbed in loculus IV, is inscribed 'Yehosef bar Qayafa'' on the back of a long side and 'Yehosef bar Qafa'' on a narrow side.

The Joseph ossuary held bones of six people: two infants, a child of 2-5, a youth of 13-18, an adult woman and a man of about 60 (anthropology by Joseph Zias). Whether that man is the high priest Joseph Caiaphas of Josephus and the Gospels is the open question; Greenhut himself framed it as a question, not a finding.

What the text says (summary)

Yehosef bar Qayafa' (long side) / Yehosef bar Qafa' (narrow side) = 'Joseph son of Caiaphas'Literal rendering (this encyclopedia) of the transliteration printed by Z. Greenhut, BAR 18:5 (1992)

What the evidence shows

  • Twelve ossuaries in one small family kokhim cave; five inscribed with names.
  • Two inscriptions spell the name differently on the same box: QYP' (Qayafa') and QP' (Qafa').
  • A second ossuary is inscribed only 'Qafa''.
  • The Joseph box is the most elaborately carved in the cave (double rosette circles).
  • Bones of six individuals in the Joseph box, including a male of about 60; the other 'Qafa'' box held five individuals and no elderly male.
  • Bronze coin of Agrippa I (42/43 CE) in a woman's skull in the 'Miriam daughter of Simon' ossuary.
  • Two iron nails were recorded in the 1990 excavation (one inside an ossuary, one on the floor of a kokh); the IAA later stated they had been misplaced (as reported in Shimron et al. 2020).
  • No title (priest, high priest) appears on either Caiaphas ossuary.

Open questions

  • Identity of the 60-year-old man: Greenhut (BAR 18:5, 1992) asks whether the ornate box and the name make him the high priest, but states it is 'another question'; the inscriptions carry no priestly title, and the box contained six people.
  • Reading of the name: the two spellings QYP' and QP' on one box are published by Greenhut and Reich (1992). W. Horbury, 'The Caiaphas Ossuaries and Joseph Caiaphas', PEQ 126 (1994) 32-48, re-examined the inscriptions and their relation to the Josephus name; the full argument is not accessible for this entry, so its specific reading is not reported here.
  • The nails: Shimron, Deutsch, Schoch and Gutkin (Archaeological Discovery 8, 2020) argue from matching sediment, flowstone and microbial signatures that two unprovenanced nails in the Tel Aviv University anthropology collection are the lost Caiaphas-tomb nails and bear crucifixion-related bone and wood; the IAA (as quoted in that paper) says the university nails have a different provenance and the tomb nails are simply misplaced.
  • Z. Lewy (Archaeology & Anthropology: Open Access 4:2, 2021) reads the carved motifs of ossuary IAA 1991-465 in the same tomb as a messianic palm-scion symbol for Jesus; that is iconographic interpretation with no inscriptional support.

Since 2024

  • 2020-07Shimron et al. publish petrochemical study claiming two nails in a Tel Aviv University collection are the lost nails from this tomb and were used in a crucifixion; IAA disputes the provenance. [5]

The Find

  • Date1st century CE (Second Temple period, before 70 CE)
  • How datedSecondary burial in ossuaries is a Jerusalem practice of roughly the last century before the Roman destruction (Greenhut, BAR 1992). In the same cave a bronze coin of Herod Agrippa I dated 42/43 CE was found in the skull of a woman in the ossuary inscribed 'Miriam daughter of Simon' (Greenhut 1992), giving a fixed point for use of the tomb. No inscription on the Joseph ossuary carries a date.
  • DiscoveredLate November / December 1990, Found by workmen building a water park; excavated for the Israel Antiquities Authority by Zvi Greenhut, Burial cave in the Peace Forest below the Haas Promenade (North Talpiyot), south Jerusalem
  • Now heldIsrael Museum, Jerusalem (Israel Antiquities Authority collection); the bones were reburied on the Mount of Olives
  • MaterialLimestone, chip-carved decoration
  • ScriptJewish (square) Aramaic script, incised
  • LanguageAramaic personal name formula ('X son of Y')

Biblical Passages

  • Matt 26:3, 57Names 'Caiaphas' as high priest at whose house Jesus was examined. The ossuary gives the same family name; it does not carry the title.
  • John 11:49-51; 18:13-14, 24, 28Caiaphas as high priest 'that year', son-in-law of Annas. The ossuary adds nothing about office or kinship to Annas.
  • Luke 3:2; Acts 4:6Caiaphas named with Annas in the priestly leadership.
  • Josephus, Ant. 18.35 (18.2.2)Josephus gives the personal name: 'Joseph, who was also called Caiaphas'. The ossuary's 'Yehosef bar Qayafa'' matches the pair Joseph + Caiaphas; note it reads 'son of Caiaphas', i.e. Caiaphas as a family name.
