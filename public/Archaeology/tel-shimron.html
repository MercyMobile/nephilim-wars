Tel Shimron cultic megastructure and favissa

Middle Bronze Age monumental complex on the acropolis of a Jezreel Valley city · c. 1750-1650 BCE

Overview

Tel Shimron is a large mound on the northwestern edge of the Jezreel Valley, excavated since 2017 by Wheaton College and Tel Aviv University under Daniel Master and Mario Martin. Their published report for the 2017 and 2019 seasons describes, on the eastern acropolis (Grid 33), 'walls built of huge boulders' supporting a multi-story structure and large mudbrick walls running parallel to the slope, between which 'tens of thousands of bones and pottery fragments were recovered', including miniature vessels and several 'bowl with seven cups' vessels of a type known from the high place at Nahariya.

In November 2024 the excavators announced that these elements belonged to one monumental complex: a cone-shaped, white-chalk-surfaced structure rising nearly 20 feet (about 6 m) above the ancient mound, and an open-air favissa (a pit for discarded offerings) of over 700 square feet with thick mudbrick walls and two staircases. The favissa held about 40,000 animal bones (mainly cattle, sheep and goat, burned at high temperatures) and about 57,000 potsherds, with two bronze bull figurines and miniature jugs and bowls. A corbelled vault found in an earlier season is part of the same complex.

The site's ancient name is Shimron (Hebrew Bible, Masoretic Text) or Shim'on (Old Greek). A 2026 Tel Aviv article by Deirdre Fulton and Michelle Hunt argues that the form without /r/ is the older one. The monumental complex is Middle Bronze Age, centuries before any setting the book of Joshua describes.

What the evidence shows

  • Grid 33 (eastern acropolis): 'walls built of huge boulders supported a multi-story structure'; large mudbrick walls parallel to the slope; 'tens of thousands of bones and pottery fragments' between them (Master and Martin, HA 2021).
  • Grid 33 pottery includes miniature vessels and multiple 'bowl with seven cups' forms, compared by the excavators to the cultic high place at Nahariya (HA 2021).
  • Grid 92 domestic phases: radiocarbon places them in MB IIB (1750-1650 BCE) and MB IIC (1650-1550 BCE); Grid 33 pottery is contemporary with Grid 92 c. 1750 BCE (HA 2021).
  • Cone-shaped structure 'rising nearly 20 feet above the ancient mound', surfaced in white chalk (BAS 2025, reporting the excavators).
  • Favissa 'over 700-square-foot', originally unroofed, thick mudbrick walls, two staircases; about 40,000 animal bones (cattle, sheep, goat, burned at high temperature) and about 57,000 sherds; two bronze bull figurines; miniature jugs and bowls (BAS 2025).
  • A corbelled vault 'had been discovered in an earlier excavation season before the team realized that the entire complex was part of a single megastructure' (BAS 2025).
  • A hematite cylinder seal of North Syrian style, MB IIA-B, came from the first season (Rossberger, BASOR 385, 2021).
  • Later occupation on the mound includes an 11th-century BCE silo with whole vessels comparable to Megiddo VI, a Persian-Hellenistic village with an Antiochus III coin hoard abandoned in the mid-2nd c. BCE, and Roman houses with ritual baths (HA 2021).
  • No inscription naming the site has been reported from the excavation.

Open questions

  • Function of the complex. Daniel Master (quoted by BAS 2025) calls it 'very much an intentional, monumental thing designed to be seen from very far away'; the excavators read the burned animal bones, miniature vessels and seven-cup bowls as cultic (sacrificial) refuse. The full final publication of the megastructure was not located; the reading so far rests on the 2021 preliminary report and press interviews.
  • Date. The preliminary report ties the acropolis pottery to c. 1750 BCE via radiocarbon from Grid 92; press coverage gives 'around 1800 BCE'. No radiocarbon dates from the favissa itself were seen.
  • Name of the city. The Masoretic Text of Joshua 11:1, 12:20 and 19:15 reads Shimron; the Old Greek reads Shim'on. Fulton and Hunt (Tel Aviv 53:1, 2026: 103-116) argue from ancient Near Eastern sources and regional memory that the name lacked the /r/, and that the MT form is a scribal harmonisation with Shimron son of Issachar (Gen 46; Num 26; 1 Chr 7).

The Find

  • DateMiddle Bronze Age; the monumental acropolis phase is contemporary with the domestic phase radiocarbon-dated to MB IIB (c. 1750-1650 BCE). Press reports round this to 'around 1800 BCE'.
  • How datedExcavation report (Master and Martin, Hadashot Arkheologiyot 2021): radiocarbon evidence places the earlier domestic phase in Grid 92 in MB IIB (1750-1650 BCE) and the later in MB IIC (1650-1550 BCE); the pottery from the monumental Grid 33 is contemporary with Grid 92 (c. 1750 BCE). Radiocarbon sampling by E. Boaretto. The 'c. 1800 BCE' in BAS/Haaretz coverage is not the report's figure.
