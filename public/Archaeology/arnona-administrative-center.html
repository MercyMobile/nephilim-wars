Mordot Arnona royal administrative center

Judahite administrative site with lmlk and 'private' stamped jar handles · late 8th - mid 7th century BCE

Overview

Mordot Arnona is a hill c. 750 m northeast of Ramat Rahel on the southern edge of modern Jerusalem, excavated by the Israel Antiquities Authority as a salvage dig. In July 2020 the IAA announced a large structure with ashlar walls, covered by an artificial stone mound, and more than 120 jar handles bearing seal impressions in Hebrew script, including lmlk ('to the king') stamps naming Hebron, Ziph, Socoh and Mmst, and stamps with personal names.

The excavators' 2022 site report (Tel Aviv 49) describes nine building and construction phases from the 9th century BCE to the 4th century CE, with the site functioning as an administrative hub, marked by monumental architecture and many stamped handles, from the late 8th to the mid 7th century BCE, and afterwards as a farming settlement. Their 2024 article in 'Atiqot 110 publishes 17 handles with 'private' stamp impressions, alongside 124 lmlk-stamped handles and 33 handles incised with concentric circles.

As reported by the Times of Israel (Oct 2024), the excavators found an earlier administrative building destroyed down to its foundations and a second one built over it, with stones of the first reused in a platform; the 17 private-name stamps come only from the earlier, destroyed building. They read the destroyed building as belonging to Hezekiah's preparations before Sennacherib's 701 BCE campaign and the rebuilt one as serving the tax/tribute system after it.

What the evidence shows

  • Location: c. 750 m northeast of Ramat Rahel, eastern outskirts of Arnona, Jerusalem ('Atiqot 110 abstract; Tel Aviv 49 abstract).
  • Nine building and construction phases from the Late Iron IIA (9th c. BCE) to the Late Roman period (4th c. CE) (Sapir et al. 2022).
  • Administrative phase late 8th - first half of 7th c. BCE, shown by monumental structures and dozens of stamped handles (Sapir et al. 2024 abstract).
  • Published handle corpus: 17 'private' stamp impressions, 124 lmlk stamps, 33 incised concentric circles (Sapir, Ben-Ari, Koch and Lipschits 2024).
  • Private-name stamps reported in 2020 include Naham Abdi, Naham Hatzlihu, Meshalem Elnatan, Zafan Abmetz, Shaneah Azaria, Shalem Acha and Shivna Shachar (IAA/Friends of the IAA transliterations, 22 July 2020); the 2024 press adds Menachem Yubna, Peqach Tavra and Tzophen Azaryahu (Times of Israel).
  • lmlk stamps name the four places Hebron, Ziph, Socoh and Mmst (IAA 2020).
  • Two superimposed administrative buildings; the first destroyed to its foundations, the second built on its ruins reusing its stones (excavators via Times of Israel, 10 Oct 2024).
  • The 17 private-name handles were found only in the earlier, destroyed building (excavators via Times of Israel, 10 Oct 2024).
  • In 2020 the IAA described a structure with ashlar walls covered by an artificial stone mound c. 20 m high over some seven dunams (Friends of the IAA release).

Open questions

  • What destroyed the first building, and when? The excavators (Sapir, Ben-Ari and colleagues, as reported by Times of Israel, Oct 2024) tie the destruction and rebuilding to Sennacherib's 701 BCE campaign and read the rebuilding as an Assyrian-era statement of 'who is really in charge'. The evidence cited is the sequence (destroyed building, rebuilt one) and the restriction of private-name stamps to the earlier phase. The published abstracts located here do not state an absolute date (e.g. radiocarbon) for the destruction; the 701 link is an interpretation from the stamp typology and regional history.
  • Function of the private-name stamps. Sapir, Ben-Ari, Koch and Lipschits ('Atiqot 110, 2024) discuss their role 'on the eve of Sennacherib's campaign'; Ben-Ari (Times of Israel 2024) reads the named men as officials receiving produce, 'likely olive oil or wine', later diverted to Assyria. The stamps themselves carry only personal names (and patronymics); what office these men held is not written on them.
  • Relationship to Ramat Rahel, 750 m away. Sapir, Ben-Ari, Freud and Lipschits (Tel Aviv 49, 2022) examine how Mordot Arnona and Ramat Rahel together contributed to the political and economic organization of Judah's last 150 years. How the two sites divided the work of collection and storage is not settled in the sources located.

Corrected from the earlier edition of this page
  • The widely repeated 2020 figure of '120 stamped handles' is superseded: the 2024 'Atiqot publication gives 17 private-stamped and 124 lmlk-stamped handles (141 stamped) plus 33 handles with incised concentric circles (Sapir et al. 2024 abstract).
  • The Oct 2024 press figure of '180 handles' does not match the published count; the 'Atiqot 2024 totals come to 174 handles of the three types, of which the 33 concentric-circle handles are incised, not stamped (Sapir et al. 2024 abstract). The source of the 180 figure was not given.
  • Descriptions of 'two monumental 8th-century buildings' (Times of Israel 2024 wording) blur the sequence: the site's administrative phase runs from the late 8th into the first half of the 7th century BCE (Sapir et al. 2022, 2024), and on the excavators' own reading the second building was built after the 701 BCE destruction, i.e. at the very end of the 8th century or in the 7th.
  • Headlines calling it proof of the 'tribute system after biblical revolt' present an interpretation as a finding: what the ground shows is a destroyed building, a rebuilt one, and a shift from private-name to lmlk/concentric-circle marking; the link to Assyrian tribute is the excavators' reading (Times of Israel 2024).

The Find

  • DateAdministrative phase: late 8th to first half of the 7th century BCE; the site overall runs from the Late Iron IIA (9th century BCE) to the Late Roman period (4th century CE)
  • How datedStratigraphy (nine building and construction phases, per Sapir et al., Tel Aviv 2022) and the stamped jar handles: lmlk ('belonging to the king') stamps, 'private' name stamps and incised concentric circles, all types dated by their occurrence in destruction layers of Sennacherib's 701 BCE campaign elsewhere in Judah and in 7th-century contexts. The link of the destroyed first building to 701 BCE is the excavators' interpretation.
  • DiscoveredSalvage excavations announced July 2020 (IAA); results published 2022 and 2024, Israel Antiquities Authority salvage excavation directed by Neria Sapir and Nathan Ben-Ari (with Benyamin Storchan per Times of Israel 2024); finds studied with Oded Lipschits (Tel Aviv University), Liora Freud and Ido Koch, Mordot Arnona, on the eastern edge of the Arnona neighbourhood of Jerusalem, c. 750 m northeast of Ramat Rahel, near the US Embassy; excavated ahead of a residential development
  • Now heldSite excavated in salvage work ahead of construction (now the Mordot Arnona residential neighbourhood); finds held by the Israel Antiquities Authority. Whether any architecture was preserved in place was not stated in the sources located.

Biblical Passages

  • 2 Kings 18:7, 13-16Hezekiah rebels against Assyria; Sennacherib takes the fortified cities of Judah; Hezekiah pays tribute of silver and gold. The excavators read the destroyed first building and the rebuilt second as the before-and-after of this event. Kings speaks of silver and gold tribute; the Arnona handles are jars for agricultural produce, so the correspondence is to the administrative system, not to the tribute payment described.
  • 2 Chronicles 32:27-29Hezekiah builds 'storehouses also for the increase of corn, and wine, and oil' (KJV v. 28). Arnona is a storage/administrative site of his era with stamped storage-jar handles; no text names this site.
  • 2 Kings 21:1Manasseh reigns 55 years after Hezekiah; the rebuilt phase of the first half of the 7th century BCE falls in his reign by the conventional chronology. The Bible says nothing about his administration of produce.
