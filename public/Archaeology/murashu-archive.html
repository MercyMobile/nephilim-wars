Murashu Archive

Cuneiform business archive of a Nippur family under Artaxerxes I and Darius II · c. 454-404 BCE

Overview

In 1893 the Penn expedition at Nippur cleared a small collapsed room and found about 730 clay tablets lying on or near its floor. They were the business records of a Nippur family, the descendants of Murashu son of Hatin, active under the Persian kings Artaxerxes I and Darius II. Hilprecht and Clay published a large part of them in 1898-1912; Stolper (1985) and Donbaz and Stolper (1997, the Istanbul tablets) published most of the rest.

The Murashus were agricultural middlemen in the Persian 'land-for-service' system. Crown land around Nippur was allotted to military and service units (hatru), each under a foreman (shaknu). The Murashus leased these holdings, supplied water, seed and oxen, advanced silver to pay the holders' taxes, took pledges of land, and collected rents in barley and dates. The tablets are promissory notes, receipts, leases and similar legal texts.

The archive names people of many origins living in the Nippur countryside: Babylonians, Persians, Arameans and others. Among them are Judeans, identified mainly by names formed with the divine name Yahweh. Alstola (2020) counts 61 Judean individuals in 64 documents, from 452 BCE (year 13 of Artaxerxes I) to 413 BCE (year 11 of Darius II), mostly as farmers dealing with the Murashus, as witnesses, and a few as minor officials. The tablets are not written in Aramaic and are not the records of a Jewish family.

What the text says (summary)

Legal-economic formulae, not narrative. A typical tablet records that a named landholder of a service unit owes the Murashu firm a quantity of barley or dates as rent for a plot on a named canal, or receives silver to pay his tax obligation, and lists witnesses and the scribe, followed by the date in the regnal year of Artaxerxes or Darius. Judeans appear by name in these roles (e.g. Pili-Yama son of Shillimu as witness in several Nippur documents of year 34 of Artaxerxes I).Summary based on Hilprecht and Clay, BE IX (1898) introduction, and Alstola, Judeans in Babylonia (2020), ch. 5

What the evidence shows

  • Hilprecht 1898: 'At the end of May, 1893 ... discovered a room (5.5 X 2.75 m. wide) about 6 m. below the surface'; 730 tablets gathered from the floor in a few hours.
  • Tablets dated in the reigns of Artaxerxes I and Darius II; BE IX's 120 texts were written by 41 different scribes (Hilprecht 1898).
  • Main span 454-414 BCE with a handful of related documents to 404 BCE; peak activity 425-417 BCE (Alstola 2020, citing Donbaz and Stolper 1997).
  • Chief protagonists: Enlil-shum-iddin (active 445/444-421 BCE) and his nephew Rimut-Ninurta (429-415/414 BCE); the family's names honour Enlil and Ninurta, gods of Nippur (Alstola 2020).
  • 61 Judean individuals in 64 documents; earliest BE 9 3 (452 BCE), latest PBS 2/1 148 (413 BCE) (Alstola 2020).
  • Hilprecht (1898) listed names he read as Hebrew, among them Gadaliama (Gedaliah), Hananiama (Hananiah), Hagga (Haggai), Shabbatai and Zabdiia, noting that many were 'known from the Old Testament, especially from the books of Ezra and Nehemiah'.
  • A Judean served as shaknu (foreman) of the shushanus of the storehouse (BE 10 65); Judean-held bow and horse lands belong to hatrus of sepirus (scribes), e.g. PBS 2/1 89, 218 (Alstola 2020).
  • Some tablets carry Aramaic dockets summarizing the content (Hilprecht 1898, e.g. no. 71).
  • Naming (Alstola 2020, Table 3): of 41 Judean fathers' names, 46% Yahwistic, 24% other West Semitic, 22% Akkadian, 2% Iranian; of 60 Judean first names, 67% Yahwistic, 25% West Semitic, 5% Akkadian, 3% Iranian.
  • The archive mentions the Kabaru canal (river Chebar): servants of the official Mannukiya were paid for carrying barley from Nippur to the Kabaru canal (Alstola 2020, ch. 5).

Open questions

  • Who counts as a Judean? Alstola (Judeans in Babylonia, 2020) identifies Judeans by names containing the Yahwistic element (spelled Ia-a-hu-/-ia-a-ma in cuneiform) and their close kin, and discusses borderline names (e.g. Ardia, Zabdia) that some treat as Yahwistic and others do not. Hilprecht's 1898 list was broader and included names that later work does not accept as Yahwistic. The count of Judeans depends on this rule.
  • Assimilated or distinct? Alstola (2020, Table 3), following an observation by E. J. Bickerman (1978), finds sons more often bore Yahwistic names (67% of 60 first names) than their fathers did (46% of 41 patronymics), while some Judeans bore Akkadian, other West Semitic and even Iranian names (the last in families dealing with Persian officials). That pattern runs against a simple story of steady assimilation. The tablets record transactions, not worship, so they are silent on Sabbath, temple or ties to Jerusalem.
  • End of the archive: Stolper (Entrepreneurs and Empire, 1985) and van Driel (1989) differed on when and why the archive stops, as Alstola (2020, n. 877) notes. The room's field record is thin: no excavation report was published, only Haynes's notes and letters.

The Find

  • DateMain span 454-414 BCE, with a few related documents to 404 BCE (Alstola 2020); Hilprecht (1898) gives the reigns of Artaxerxes I and Darius II
  • How datedEvery contract carries a regnal date (day, month, year of Artaxerxes I or Darius II) in its colophon; the dates are internal to the tablets.
  • DiscoveredEnd of May / June 1893, University of Pennsylvania Babylonian Expedition, field director John Henry Haynes, A single room (5.5 x 2.75 m) about 6 m below the surface on the north-western ridge ('Camp Hill') of Nippur, west of the Inanna temple and Ekur, Iraq
  • Now heldMainly Istanbul Archaeological Museum and the Penn Museum (University of Pennsylvania Museum of Archaeology and Anthropology), Philadelphia; a few in Jena, Yale, the British Museum and elsewhere
  • MaterialClay tablets (plus about twenty clay bullae); many carry seal impressions and some have short Aramaic epigraphs (dockets)
  • DimensionsAbout 730 tablets gathered from the room in 1893 (Hilprecht 1898); about 750 texts counted in the archive today (Alstola 2020)
  • ScriptNeo-/Late Babylonian cuneiform, with brief Aramaic dockets in alphabetic script on some tablets
  • LanguageAkkadian (Late Babylonian), with Aramaic notes

Biblical Passages

  • Ezra 2; Neh 7The return lists show Judeans leaving Babylonia; the Murashu texts show Judeans who stayed in the Nippur countryside a century after the deportations, under Artaxerxes I and Darius II. Several names in the tablets (e.g. Hananiah, Gedaliah, Haggai) are the same names borne by people in Ezra-Nehemiah, not the same individuals.
  • Neh 1:1-2:8Nehemiah serves Artaxerxes I; the archive's main span (454-414 BCE) is the same reign and the next, and documents the tax and land system of the Persian crown in Babylonia at the time.
  • Ezek 1:1-3; 3:15; Ezra 8:15-17Exiles settled by canals in Babylonia (the Chebar, Tel-abib, the Ahava canal). The Murashu archive itself names the Kabaru canal, the Babylonian form of 'Chebar', as the waterway to which barley from Nippur was shipped (Alstola 2020): the same canal Ezekiel names, attested in the 5th-century business records.
