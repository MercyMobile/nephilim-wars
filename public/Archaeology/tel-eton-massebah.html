Concealed standing stone at Tel 'Eton

Household standing stone laid down and built into a platform before the Assyrian destruction · late 8th century BCE

Overview

Building 101 at Tel 'Eton is a large four-space ("four-room") house on the summit of the mound, excavated over ten seasons (2006-2015) and sealed under a massive late 8th century BCE destruction layer. The excavators sifted all sediment and excavated much of the floor on a 20 x 20 cm grid, which let them reconstruct the use of every room on the eve of destruction.

In Room 101B, the largest and one of the innermost rooms, the 2014 season found a dense layer of small-to-medium stones (F1489) with one exceptionally large worked stone in it. In 2015 the layer turned out to be a platform covering only the south-western part of the room, not collapse. The large stone (in two pieces, F1476) was the only worked stone in it, far larger than the rest, and it lay on a continuous foundation (F1387) that crosses the room, the only place in the building where such a foundation crosses a room rather than continuing a wall.

Avraham Faust (Jerusalem Journal of Archaeology 9, 2026) reads the stone as a massebah that originally stood on that foundation near wall F1381, positioned so that it was visible from the building's entrance and much of the courtyard, and that was later laid down and 'concealed' inside a platform built around it. He proposes, as a plausible context, the cult changes attributed to Hezekiah (2 Kgs 18:4, 22), while stating that the date evidence allows but does not prove that link.

What the evidence shows

  • Building 101 was excavated over 10 seasons (2006-2015); walls stood 1.0-1.5 m high; hundreds of vessels were found in place (Faust 2026: 35).
  • Room 101B measures 16.7 sq m, the largest in the building (other rooms 3.8-13.5 sq m) (Faust 2026: 40, 42).
  • The stone layer F1489 covers only the south-western part of Room 101B; the floor beneath it held no complete vessels, unlike most other rooms (Faust 2026: 36-38).
  • The two large fragments F1476 are one worked stone, ca. 140 x 70 x 36 cm and ca. 750 kg; they are the only worked stones in the layer (Faust 2026: 38).
  • The large stone lies on continuous foundation F1387, which crosses the room; this is the only such crossing recorded in the building (Faust 2026: 38-39).
  • The platform was in use at the time of the Assyrian destruction; a cooking pot was found on top of it (Faust 2026: 45).
  • The excavator judges the break in the stone accidental, occurring when it was laid down (Faust 2026: n. 6).
  • Large chunks of wood were found below the platform; the wood was not identified (Faust 2026: n. 7).
  • A bench runs along the room's southern wall (Faust 2026: n. 3).
  • Radiocarbon samples from a foundation deposit and floor make-up place the building's earliest phase in the late 11th-10th century BCE (Faust and Sapir 2018 abstract).

Open questions

  • Whether the stone was a cult object at all. Faust (JJAR 9, 2026) reads it as a massebah because it had no practical function, sat on a foundation built for it, and was placed to be seen from the entrance. The article records no inscription, carving or offering deposit on the stone itself; the cultic reading rests on size, position and the special foundation (our observation from the published description).
  • When and by whom it was laid down. Faust (2026: 45) states only that it happened before the destruction and that how long before 'we cannot, at this stage, determine'; the link to Hezekiah is his proposal from the date range plus similar changes at Beersheba, Arad and Lachish. He himself says the Arad and Lachish evidence is 'not unequivocal' and that the changes 'need not have been contemporaneous' (2026: 48, 50).
  • Reform or war. Faust (2026: 49) reports that Edelman (2008), Fried (2002) and Handy (1988) explain dismantled cult places as consequences of, or precautions against, Sennacherib's 701 BCE campaign. Faust answers that at Beersheba and Tel 'Eton the dismantled items were built into structures that were in use when the Assyrians arrived, so the dismantling preceded the campaign. These opposing works have not been reviewed directly for this entry; their positions are given as Faust reports them.
  • Date of the building's first phase. Faust and Sapir (Radiocarbon 60, 2018) put its founding in the late 11th-10th century BCE from 14C and argue for an 'old-house effect'; their 2021 paper in Palestine Exploration Quarterly 153 is titled as a response to a Low Chronology critique of that date. The critique itself has not been located for this entry.

The Find

  • DateStone erected in the building's first phase (the building itself dated late 11th-10th century BCE by radiocarbon); laid down and embedded in a platform at an undetermined time before the Assyrian destruction of the late 8th century BCE
  • How datedStratigraphy: the platform (F1489) holding the laid-down stone was in use when Building 101 was destroyed, and a cooking pot lay on top of it (Faust 2026: 45). The destruction layer is attributed by the excavator to the Assyrian army in the late 8th century BCE. Faust states: 'how long before the destruction did the dismantling take place, we cannot, at this stage, determine' (2026: 45), and notes radiocarbon is imprecise for the late 8th century because of the Hallstatt plateau (2026: n. 14). The building's founding date rests on 14C samples from a foundation deposit and floor make-up (Faust and Sapir, Radiocarbon 60, 2018).
  • DiscoveredStone layer and the larger fragment exposed in the 2014 season; rest of Room 101B and the second fragment in 2015, Tel 'Eton Archaeological Project, Bar-Ilan University, directed by Avraham Faust, Room 101B (the largest room, 16.7 sq m, south-western part) of Building 101, the 'governor's residency', Area A on top of Tel 'Eton, south-eastern Judean Shephelah
  • Now heldIn situ as excavated (the large fragments were left in place; Faust 2026 figs. 6, 8); no museum record located
  • MaterialWorked stone (lithology not given in the article)
  • Dimensionsca. 140 x 70 x 36 cm, ca. 750 kg; broken into two fragments that match in dimension and indentation (Faust 2026: 38)

Biblical Passages

  • 2 Kings 18:4, 22Hezekiah 'removed the high places, and brake the images (Hebrew matstsebot, standing stones), and cut down the groves' (KJV). The find is a standing stone taken out of use before the late 8th-century destruction. Its date range fits Hezekiah's reign; nothing on the site names a king or a decree (Faust's proposal, labelled as such).
  • 2 Chronicles 31:1Standing stones broken throughout Judah and Benjamin after Hezekiah's Passover. At Tel 'Eton the stone was not smashed but laid down whole (the break judged accidental) and enclosed in a platform; that differs from 'brake the images in pieces' (KJV) in the text.
  • Genesis 28:18; 35:14; Exodus 24:4; Joshua 24:26-27Standing stones set up by patriarchs and leaders as memorials; cited by BAS as background for what a massebah was. The Tel 'Eton stone stood inside a house, a domestic setting none of these texts describes.
  • Joshua 10:3, 34-35; 15:39Eglon, a Shephelah city of Judah. BAS calls Tel 'Eton 'likely biblical Eglon'; the 2026 article does not argue the identification, and no inscription from the site names it.
