Nuzi Tablets

Private and official cuneiform records from a Hurrian town near Kirkuk, Iraq · 15th-14th century BCE

Overview

Nuzi (Yorghan Tepe, near Kirkuk) was excavated from 1925 by Edward Chiera for the Iraq Museum and the American School of Oriental Research in Baghdad, and from 1927 with Harvard University; Speiser wrote in 1930 that the Nuzi tablets were already 'one of the largest collections of cuneiform tablets dug up on a single site.' The texts were published in the series 'Joint Expedition with the Iraq Museum at Nuzi' (from 1927) and 'Excavations at Nuzi' (Harvard Semitic Series, from 1929).

The tablets are mostly private legal and economic records of families in a Hurrian town of the kingdom of Arrapha: adoptions (including 'sale-adoptions' used to transfer land), wills, marriage contracts, court cases and loans. They let one follow some families over several generations.

From the 1920s-1950s, scholars compared Nuzi customs with the patriarchal stories in Genesis - a barren wife giving her husband a concubine, adoption of an heir, household gods and inheritance. Later studies (T. L. Thompson 1974; M. Greenberg 1962 on the teraphim) re-examined those parallels text by text.

In its own words

If Gilimninu bears (children), Shennima shall not take another wife; and if Gilimninu does not bear, Gilimninu a woman of the Lullu as wife for Shennima shall take. As for (the concubine's) offspring, Gilimninu shall [not] send (them) away.E. A. Speiser, 'New Kirkuk Documents Relating to Family Laws,' AASOR 10 (1930), text 2 (= HSS V 67), lines 16-23, p. 32

What the evidence shows

  • HSS V 67 (Speiser text 2): an adoption tied to a marriage. If the wife Gilimninu is childless she must provide her husband a Lullu woman; the concubine's children may not be expelled; if Gilimninu has children the husband may not take another wife (Speiser 1930).
  • Same text: Shurihil's natural son, if born later, would be 'firstborn' and take a double share; the adopted Shennima becomes second heir (Speiser 1930, lines 7-11).
  • Same text: a handmaid is also given to Gilimninu, with a supervisor assigned (lines 35-37; the handmaid's name is only partly legible in the scan).
  • Speiser (1930) grouped 40 texts from HSS V as real adoptions, sale-adoptions, wills, and marriage records.
  • Local Akkadian shows Hurrian influence; Speiser notes pronunciation 'doubtless under the influence of Hurrian' in ancient Arrapha (1930 notes).

Open questions

  • Do Nuzi customs date the patriarchs? The 1920s-50s school (e.g. C. J. Gadd, 'Tablets from Kirkuk', RA 23, 1926, cited by Speiser; E. A. Speiser 1930) read Nuzi family law as the background of Genesis 15-31. T. L. Thompson (The Historicity of the Patriarchal Narratives, BZAW 133, 1974, ch. 10 'Nuzi and the Patriarchal Narratives', pp. 196-297) re-examined each proposed parallel and argued that several rest on misreadings of the tablets or occur in other periods too. The HSS V 67 clause is real; whether it fixes the date of Genesis is the argument.
  • Rachel's teraphim (Gen 31): the proposal that Nuzi household gods served as title to inheritance was re-examined by M. Greenberg ('Another Look at Rachel's Theft of the Teraphim,' JBL 81, 1962) and by M. A. Morrison ('The Jacob and Laban Narrative in Light of Near Eastern Sources,' BA 46, 1983, 155-164). The texts of these articles were not available for this entry, so their exact conclusions are not stated here.

The Find

  • DateMid-2nd millennium BCE, Mitanni period (conventionally 15th-14th century BCE)
  • How datedTie-in to the Mitanni king Saushshatar, whose letter occurs in the Nuzi material (E. A. Speiser, 'A Letter of Saushshatar and the Date of the Kirkuk Tablets,' JAOS 49, 1929), plus the generations of families traced through the archives. The tablets carry no absolute dates.
  • DiscoveredExcavation begun 1925; continued from 1927 through the early 1930s, Edward Chiera (1925, Iraq Museum + American School of Oriental Research in Baghdad; 1927, with Harvard University), then R. H. Pfeiffer and R. F. S. Starr as directors; University of Pennsylvania Museum later associated, Yorghan Tepe (ancient Nuzi), near Kirkuk, Iraq; the kingdom of Arrapha
  • Now heldDivided between the Iraq Museum and the American partner institutions of the joint expeditions; the present repositories of specific tablets are not confirmed in a published museum record
  • MaterialClay tablets
  • ScriptCuneiform
  • LanguageAkkadian, local dialect with heavy Hurrian influence (many Hurrian personal names and terms)

Biblical Passages

  • Genesis 16:1-4; 30:1-13HSS V 67 obliges a childless wife to give her husband a slave-concubine and protects that woman's children - the same arrangement Sarah makes with Hagar and Rachel and Leah make with Bilhah and Zilpah. That is a documented legal practice, not a Nuzi text about the patriarchs.
  • Genesis 15:2-4The same tablet has an adopted heir displaced to second rank if a natural son is born, comparable to Abram's words about Eliezer and the promise of a son. Correspondence of custom; our reading.
  • Genesis 31:19-35Proposed link between Nuzi household gods and inheritance rights; disputed (Greenberg 1962; Morrison 1983). Not established from sources read here.
