Jehohanan Crucifixion Remains

Heel bone pierced by an iron nail, from an ossuary in a Jerusalem tomb · 1st century CE

Overview

In 1968 contractors building north of Jerusalem opened Jewish rock-cut tombs at Giv'at ha-Mivtar. Vassilios Tzaferis excavated them (IEJ 20, 1970). In one ossuary, inscribed 'Jehohanan son of HGQWL', were the bones of a man in his twenties whose right heel bone was still transfixed by an iron nail.

The nail had entered the side of the heel and its tip had bent back on itself, which Zias reads as the nail having struck a knot in the upright so that it could not be pulled out when the body was taken down. Olive wood lay between the nail head and the bone, read as a small wooden plaque under the nail head.

Nicu Haas's 1970 anatomical report reported the nail as 17-18 cm and reconstructed both heels as fixed by one nail. Zias and Sekeles's 1985 re-examination measured the nail at 11.5 cm, too short to pass through two feet, and found no traumatic injury to the forearms.

In its own words

What the evidence shows

  • Right calcaneus penetrated laterally by an iron nail, tip bent back on itself (Zias).
  • Nail length 11.5 cm (Zias & Sekeles 1985), not 17-18 cm (Haas 1970).
  • Olive-wood remains between nail head and bone.
  • No traumatic lesion on the forearms found in the 1985 re-examination.
  • Skeleton friable, fragmentary, with many post-mortem breaks.
  • Ossuary inscription gives a personal name and an unexplained patronymic/epithet HGQWL.
  • The man received normal Jewish secondary burial in a family tomb.

Open questions

  • How he was fixed to the cross: N. Haas (IEJ 20, 1970) proposed one 17-18 cm nail through both heels (as reported by Zias); J. Zias and E. Sekeles (IEJ 35, 1985) reject this because the nail is only 11.5 cm and they found no traumatic injury to the forearms, and Zias concludes the arms were bound, the feet nailed separately.
  • How many crucifixion victims survive: Zias (essay) and Gualdi-Russo et al. (Archaeological and Anthropological Sciences 11, 2019) both treat Giv'at ha-Mivtar as the only well-documented case; Gualdi-Russo et al. propose a second, 'possible' case from an isolated Roman burial at Gavello, northern Italy (excavated 2007), from a lesion on a heel bone, while stating the poor bone preservation complicates the reading. Zias explains the rarity by crucifixion nails being taken as healing amulets (Mishnah Shabbat 6:10) and by victims often being tied.
  • Meaning of HGQWL: the sources read give it untranslated; its reading (patronymic, place or nickname) is not settled in them.

The Find

  • Date1st century CE, before 70 CE
  • How datedContext: secondary burial in an inscribed ossuary in a Jewish rock-cut tomb of the Second Temple period at Giv'at ha-Mivtar; Tzaferis (BAR 1985) places the man 'shortly after the turn of the era and sometime before the Roman destruction of Jerusalem in 70 A.D.' No absolute date on the bone itself was located.
  • Discovered1968, Found by building contractors; excavated by Vassilios Tzaferis for the Israel Department of Antiquities; bones studied by Nicu Haas (1970), re-examined by Joseph Zias and Eliezer Sekeles (1985), Giv'at ha-Mivtar, a suburb north of Jerusalem
  • Now heldNot confirmed in a published museum record
  • MaterialHuman right calcaneus (heel bone) with an iron nail, olive-wood fragments; limestone ossuary
  • DimensionsNail 11.5 cm long (Zias & Sekeles 1985); Haas (1970) had reported 17-18 cm
  • ScriptOssuary inscription in Jewish (square) script
  • LanguageHebrew/Aramaic personal name

Biblical Passages

  • John 20:25-27Thomas speaks of 'the print of the nails' in Jesus' hands. The find shows nailing of the feet in 1st-century Judea; it gives no evidence about hands, and Zias's reading of this victim is that the arms were tied.
  • Luke 24:39-40'See my hands and my feet' - the Gospel implies wounds in the feet; this is the one physical example of a Judean crucifixion victim's foot being nailed.
  • Mark 15:42-46; John 19:38-42Joseph of Arimathea obtains the body and gives it Jewish burial in a rock tomb. Jehohanan too, though crucified, was released for burial in a family tomb and later placed in an ossuary - showing a crucified man could receive regular Jewish burial.
  • Deut 21:22-23Anyone hanged on a tree must be buried the same day; cited by Zias as background to Jewish handling of executed bodies.
