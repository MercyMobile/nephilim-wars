Qumran and the Dead Sea Scrolls

Manuscripts from eleven caves near Khirbet Qumran · 3rd century BCE – 1st century CE

Overview

From 1947 to 1956 about 930 manuscripts, most surviving only as fragments, were recovered from eleven caves near the ruin of Khirbet Qumran on the north-western shore of the Dead Sea (the IAA's Leon Levy Digital Library images about 930). They include copies of every book of the Hebrew Bible except Esther, other Jewish writings (Enoch, Jubilees, Tobit, Ben Sira), and texts of a particular community such as the Community Rule, the Damascus Document, the War Scroll and the pesharim (running commentaries).

The best-known single scroll is the Great Isaiah Scroll (1QIsaᵃ) from Cave 1: complete except for small damaged spots, 7.34 m long, 17 sheets sewn together, 54 columns. The Israel Museum put the whole scroll on display on 24 February 2026 for the first time since 1968. The exhibition closed after four days because of the war, and the scroll went back into a protected vault (Haaretz, 6 April 2026).

Next to the caves, Roland de Vaux excavated Khirbet Qumran itself in 1951–1956. Who lived there, and how the site relates to the scrolls, remains disputed (see questions).

What the evidence shows

  • Cave 1 yielded seven large scrolls in 1947, including 1QIsaᵃ. Caves 4 and 11 yielded thousands of fragments.
  • 1QIsaᵃ contains Isaiah 1–66 in one scroll written by two scribes, one for each half. Enoch's writing-style model dates both halves to 180–100 BCE and shows no time gap between them (Popović et al. 2025, Fig. 2).
  • Radiocarbon (Bonani et al. 1992; Jull et al. 1995) gave dates for 14 and then 18 texts that fall in the last centuries BCE and 1st century CE.
  • Popović et al. (2025): 4Q114 (Daniel 8–11) has a 2σ radiocarbon range of 230–160 BCE. Enoch dates 4Q109 (Ecclesiastes) to the 3rd century BCE.
  • The same study found that manuscripts in 'Herodian' script carry radiocarbon dates spread from the 2nd century BCE to the 2nd century CE, wider than the palaeographic scheme allowed.
  • A twelfth cave identified in 2017 held scroll jars and a scrap of blank parchment but no written scroll.

Open questions

  • Who owned the scrolls and lived at Qumran? Magness (The Archaeology of Qumran and the Dead Sea Scrolls, Eerdmans 2002; 2nd ed. 2021) reads the site as a sectarian settlement tied to the scrolls. Her evidence is the site's layout, its many miqva'ot, communal dining debris and its connection to the caves, and she rejects the villa or manor readings. Hirschfeld (Qumran in Context, Hendrickson 2004) reads Qumran as a Hasmonean fort and later a prosperous estate on the Dead Sea trade route, unconnected to a sect. He places it among contemporary Judean desert estates and Ein Feshkha.
  • How far can script-dating be trusted? Popović et al. (2025) show several manuscripts are older than their palaeographic dates, with only two younger. Some Hasmonaean and Herodian script types overlap in time rather than following one another. Their AI dates are predictions from 24 radiocarbon-dated training samples with a mean error of about 28–31 years, and experts judged 79% of the 135 predictions for undated scrolls 'realistic'.
  • Was Daniel written in the 160s BCE? Popović et al. note that the 2σ radiocarbon range for 4Q114 (230–160 BCE) overlaps the 160s date usually assigned to Daniel 8–11 on literary-historical grounds. The earliest part of that range is older than the 160s. The radiocarbon result does not by itself decide the question.

The Find

  • DateIndividual manuscripts range from the 3rd century BCE to the 1st century CE by radiocarbon and palaeography; the Qumran caves were found 1947–1956.
  • How datedAMS radiocarbon on parchment/papyrus (Bonani et al., Radiocarbon 34, 1992; Jull et al., Radiocarbon 37, 1995; Popović et al., PLOS ONE 20, 2025: 27 valid new dates from 30 samples), plus palaeography of the scripts.
  • Discovered1947 (Cave 1) to 1956 (Cave 11); new cave (Cave 12) identified 2017, Bedouin shepherds first; then Bedouin and the Jordanian Department of Antiquities, École Biblique and Palestine Archaeological Museum teams, Caves in the cliffs and marl terrace around Khirbet Qumran, north-western Dead Sea
  • Now heldMostly Israel Antiquities Authority (Dead Sea Scrolls lab, Jerusalem) and the Israel Museum's Shrine of the Book (the Cave 1 scrolls including 1QIsaᵃ). Other pieces are held elsewhere; the Copper Scroll (3Q15) is in Amman.
  • MaterialMostly parchment, some papyrus; one text (3Q15) on copper
  • Dimensions1QIsaᵃ: 7.34 m long, 25.3–27 cm high, 17 sheets, 54 columns
  • ScriptSquare Jewish (Aramaic) script, some Paleo-Hebrew, Greek
  • LanguageHebrew, Aramaic, Greek

Biblical Passages

  • Isaiah 1–661QIsaᵃ is a complete Hebrew Isaiah copied in the 2nd century BCE (Enoch estimate 180–100 BCE), about a thousand years older than the medieval Masoretic codices. It agrees with the later Hebrew text in substance, but it also has many spelling differences and a number of word-level variants.
  • Daniel 8–114Q114 preserves these chapters on a manuscript radiocarbon-dated 230–160 BCE (2σ), within about a generation of the events Daniel 11 describes, or earlier.
  • Ecclesiastes4Q109 is dated by Enoch to the 3rd century BCE.
