Tobiah Inscription

The name 'Tobiah' cut beside two cave entrances at ʿIraq al-Amir, Jordan · date disputed, 6th to 2nd century BCE

Overview

At ʿIraq al-Amir in the Wadi es-Sir, west of Amman, two tiers of caves are cut into a cliff. Beside the entrances of two of them the same five-letter name is carved in large letters: ṭwbyh, 'Tobiah'. Below the cliff stand a village and the monumental Hellenistic building Qasr al-ʿAbd. Josephus (Antiquities 12.4.11) describes a fortress built of white stone with animals carved on it, a moat, and caves 'of many furlongs in length', built by Hyrcanus son of Joseph the Tobiad, who ruled there until his suicide under Antiochus IV. The site has been identified with that fortress.

The inscription says nothing but the name. It has no title (not 'governor', not 'servant'), no patronymic and no date. Its date therefore depends entirely on how the letter shapes are placed in the development of the Aramaic and Jewish scripts. As C. C. Ji (1998) tabulates, proposals run from the 6th / early 5th century BCE (B. Mazar) to the 2nd-3rd century BCE (Clermont-Ganneau, Dalman, Vincent).

The reading itself took time to settle. Conder's Survey of Eastern Palestine (1889) records that Euting read the name as 'Turiah' ('Tyre') and de Vogüé as 'Arabiah', and Conder offered his own reading. Later epigraphers read ṭwbyh, 'Tobiah', and Ji (1998) summarizes the site's scholarship on that reading.

In its own words

ṭwbyh — 'Tobiah'Literal rendering (this encyclopedia) of the name as read in the literature summarized by Ji, Liber Annuus 48 (1998): 417-440. The inscription consists of this single name. No public-domain translation of the modern reading exists; Conder (1889) printed a different reading.

What the evidence shows

  • Two inscriptions of the one word ṭwbyh are cut near the entrances of two caves in the cliff at ʿIraq al-Amir (Ji 1998, p. 419).
  • Conder (1889, section 'Tyrus', pp. 76-77) places one 'to the right of the door, high up' at cave No. 13. His copy agreed with de Vogüé's and is backed by Lt. Mantell's photograph (Series No. 26).
  • The text is a bare personal name, with no title, patronymic or date.
  • The caves lie in two tiers, the upper reached by a rock-cut gallery about 300 yards long (Conder 1889).
  • The settlement below had occupation in the late Iron Age / Persian period: Ulvoczky (2017), restudying P. W. Lapp's original sherds, dates Stratum V to Iron IIC/Persian. Ji (1998) cites French excavators for early-Persian-period building phases and for Persian walls reused in the Hellenistic period.
  • The Zenon papyri (C.P.Jud. 1, 4-5, as cited by Ji 1998) show 3rd-century BCE Tobiads as influential Jewish landowners in Transjordan who commanded a cavalry garrison.

Open questions

  • Is this the Tobiah of Nehemiah? B. Mazar (1957, 141-142) dated the letters to the 6th or early 5th century BCE, and Albright (as cited by Ji) to the 5th, which would bring the carving near Nehemiah's time (c. 445 BCE). F. M. Cross (1961), J. Naveh (1971) and D. Gera (1990) date the script to the 4th century BCE. Clermont-Ganneau (1896), Dalman (1921) and Vincent (1923) put it in the 2nd-3rd century BCE, which would fit the Tobiads of the Zenon papyri and of Josephus (Ji 1998, nn. 37-38). The disagreement is over letter forms in a five-letter word, which is a very small sample for palaeography.
  • Was the family already at ʿIraq al-Amir in the Persian period? P. W. Lapp dated Stratum V to Iron I, with a gap before the Hellenistic period. C. C. Ji (1998) and D. Ulvoczky (2017) argue from the pottery for late Iron II / Persian occupation, which is compatible with a Persian-period Tobiah at the site. No Persian-period object at the site bears the name.
  • What was the inscription for? Tomb markers, property marks and dedicatory labels for the caves have all been proposed. The inscription gives no function, and the sources are silent.

Corrected from the earlier edition of this page
  • The old entry said the inscription names Tobiah 'as a governor'. It is the bare name ṭwbyh with no title. In Nehemiah, Tobiah's title is 'the servant (ʿebed), the Ammonite', not governor.
  • The old entry dated it firmly to the '3rd century BCE' (and 'c. 200 BCE'). Published dates range from the 6th / early 5th century BCE (Mazar) to the 2nd-3rd century BCE (Clermont-Ganneau and others), all on palaeography (Ji 1998). No single date is established.
  • The old entry called it a 'stone inscription… approximately 1 meter tall'. It is cut into the cliff face beside the cave entrances, not on a separate stone. No measurement was found in the sources read.
  • The old entry claimed it was 'written in a form of Hebrew script' and 'provides valuable insights into… administrative… practices'. It is a single personal name in early square (Aramaic-derived) script and contains no administrative content.
  • The old entry said it 'confirms the existence of' Nehemiah's Tobiah. The name and region match, but only the earliest proposed dates (Mazar, Albright) would make the carving contemporary with Nehemiah. On the 4th-2nd century dates it would belong to later members of a Tobiad family.
  • The old entry referred to 'the Tobiah Inscription' in the singular. There are two carvings of the same name, at two caves (Ji 1998, p. 419).

The Find

  • DateDisputed, by palaeography only: 2nd-3rd century BCE (Clermont-Ganneau 1896; Dalman; Vincent), 4th century BCE (Cross 1961; Naveh 1971; Gera), 5th century BCE (Albright), or 6th / early 5th century BCE (Mazar 1957), as tabulated by Ji (1998)
  • How datedLetter forms only. The inscription is a single name cut into the living rock, with no stratified context and no associated datable object. Conder (1889) dated it to Hyrcanus the Tobiad (d. 176 BCE) by linking the caves to Josephus' account, not by the letters.
  • DiscoveredKnown to 19th-century travellers. Copied by de Vogüé; recorded and photographed for the PEF Survey of Eastern Palestine (Conder, Lt. Mantell), published 1889., Melchior de Vogüé (copy); C. R. Conder and Lt. Mantell, R.E. (survey), Rock-cut cave façade (two tiers of caves) about 200 m NW of the ancient village at ʿIraq al-Amir, Wadi es-Sir, c. 17 km west of Amman
  • Now heldIn situ, cut into the cliff beside two cave entrances at ʿIraq al-Amir, Jordan
  • MaterialLetters cut into the living rock of the cliff
  • ScriptEarly Jewish/Aramaic square script (Mazar calls it among the earliest examples of the official Aramaic square script)
  • LanguageA personal name, ṭwbyh ('Tobiah', meaning 'YHWH is good'), in Hebrew/Aramaic form

Biblical Passages

  • Neh 2:10, 19; 4:3, 7; 6:1, 12-19; 13:4-9'Tobiah the servant (hāʿebed), the Ammonite' opposes Nehemiah and later lodges in a temple chamber. The inscription gives the same name, ṭwbyh, in Ammonite territory (ʿIraq al-Amir lies west of Rabbath-Ammon). The name and the region correspond. Whether this is the same man or a family name repeated over generations depends on the date.
  • Neh 6:18Tobiah's son Jehohanan married into a Jerusalem family, and Tobiah had sworn allies in Judah. Josephus' later Tobiads (Joseph, Hyrcanus) are tied to the Jerusalem priesthood. That is a family pattern across the sources, not something the inscription shows.
  • Ezra 2:60; Neh 7:62The 'children of Tobiah' among returnees who could not prove their descent. This is another biblical occurrence of the name group. It has no direct connection to the carving.
