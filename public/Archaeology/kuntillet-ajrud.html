Kuntillet 'Ajrud (Horvat Teman) inscriptions

Iron Age way-station in northeastern Sinai with ink and plaster inscriptions naming YHWH of Samaria and YHWH of Teman · c. 800 BCE

Overview

Kuntillet 'Ajrud is a small Iron Age building complex on a hill by wells in northeastern Sinai, on the Darb el-Ghazza route. Ze'ev Meshel excavated it in 1975-1976 and titled his 2012 final report 'An Iron Age II Religious Site on the Judah-Sinai Border'. The 2015 Hebrew edition, 'To YHWH of Teman and His Ashera', dropped the 'religious site' label and replaced Pirhiya Beck's chapter on the drawings with one by Tallay Ornan.

The inscriptions are the reason the site is famous. They are blessing formulas written in ink on two pithoi found in a bench room of the main building. Pithos A (KA 3.1) blesses 'by YHWH of Shomron (Samaria) and by his asherah'. Pithos B (KA 3.6, 3.9) blesses 'by YHWH of Teman and by his asherah'. A plaster text near the bench-room entrance (KA 4.2) describes a theophany in which El shines forth, the mountains melt, and Baal and 'the name of El' appear 'on the day of battle'. Four inscriptions carry the title sr 'r, 'commander of the fortress'.

Painted drawings on Pithos A include two figures that some identify as the Egyptian god Bes, and a lion beneath a tree with gazelles (as described by Smoak and Schniedewind 2019). Whether the figures are deities, and which ones, is disputed.

What the text says (summary)

[... I have blessed you] by YHWH of the Teman and by his asherah ... whatever he asks from a man, he (YHWH) will give generously ... and YHW will give to him according to his heart.Literal translation (this encyclopedia) of inscription KA 3.9 (Pithos B) from the transliteration [brktk] lyhwh.htmn wl'shrth ... kl 'shr ysh'l m'sh hnn h' ... wntn lh yhw klbbh, as published by Ahituv, Eshel and Meshel 2012 and quoted in Smoak and Schniedewind, Religions 10(3):211 (2019). No public-domain translation exists.

What the evidence shows

  • KA 3.1, red ink on Pithos A: '... I have [b]lessed you to YHWH of Shomron (Samaria) and to his 'asherah' (Ahituv et al. 2012: 87, as quoted by Smoak and Schniedewind 2019).
  • KA 3.6, Pithos B: 'Message of Amaryaw: Say to my lord, are you well? I have blessed you to YHWH of Teman and his 'asherah. May he bless you and may he guard you, and may he be with my lord' (Ahituv et al. 2012: 95, as quoted in the same).
  • KA 3.9, Pithos B: lyhwh.htmn wl'shrth, 'by YHWH of the Teman and by his asherah' (same).
  • KA 4.2, plaster near the western entrance of the bench room: names El, Baal and YHWH with theophany language ('when El shines forth ... the mountains will melt') (Ahituv et al. 2012: 110).
  • Four inscriptions carry the military title sr 'r, 'commander of the fortress': three on storage jars and one on plaster (Ahituv et al. 2015: 80-81, 113; Schniedewind 2019).
  • No temple, altar or conventional cult figurine was found. Schmidt (2016) notes that 'none of the standard diagnostics' of cult were found in the bench room (quoted by Smoak and Schniedewind 2019).
  • Personal names use the northern theophoric ending -yaw, and the pottery has been read as Israelite-influenced (Ayalon 2012). Singer-Avitz (2006) instead reads the bulk of the pottery as Judahite.
  • Radiocarbon: built c. 820-795 BCE and abandoned after 745 BCE; historical considerations may narrow this to c. 795-730/720 BCE (Finkelstein and Piasetzky 2008, abstract).

Open questions

  • What does 'his asherah' mean? Lemaire (Jerusalem Journal of Archaeology 7, 2024: 58-60) sets out four readings. (1) A goddess, Yahweh's consort: Dever (BASOR 255, 1984) and others, on the basis of Ugaritic Athirat. (2) A sanctuary: Cross (2009), Sass (2014) and Puech (2015), on the basis of Phoenician and Aramaic 'shrt meaning shrine. (3) A wooden pole standing for the goddess: Day (1992). (4) A sacred tree or grove: Na'aman and Lissovsky (Tel Aviv 35, 2008), and Lemaire, citing Deut 16:21, the Septuagint's alsos ('grove'), and Mishnah Avodah Zarah 3:7. Lemaire also notes that Ahituv translated the word as a goddess in 2008 and as 'a sacred cultic object' in 2012.
  • Was it a religious site? Meshel's 2012 report called it one. Hadley (1993) argued it has no temple, shrine or cult objects. Smoak and Schniedewind (Religions 2019) read it as a fortress and way-station whose texts include scribal practice letters. Na'aman and Lissovsky (2008) propose that the cult took place outside, around a sacred tree, and that the building served as the goddess's storehouse.
  • When was it occupied? Singer-Avitz (Tel Aviv 33, 2006) compared the pottery with Lachish III and down-dated the site to the end of the 8th century BCE. Freud (Tel Aviv 35, 2008) pointed to forms already present in Iron IIA and kept the late 9th to first half of the 8th century. Finkelstein and Piasetzky (Tel Aviv 35, 2008) used radiocarbon to put construction at c. 820-795 BCE. Singer-Avitz replied in Tel Aviv 36 (2009).
  • Who is drawn on Pithos A? Beck (1982; 2012) read the figures religiously. Ornan (2015; 2016) finds parallels for the drawings in Neo-Assyrian palace reliefs and reads the building as a royal or state outpost. Identifying the two figures as Bes, or one of them as Asherah, is not settled by the drawings themselves.

Corrected from the earlier edition of this page
  • The inscriptions are often quoted as 'Yahweh and his Asherah' with a capital A, as if a goddess's personal name were certain. The Hebrew is l'shrth, 'his asherah', with a possessive suffix. Ahituv himself translated it as 'a sacred cultic object' in the 2012 final report (Lemaire 2024: 60).
  • A 'Kuntillet Ajrud final report' ISBN 978-965-221-088-3 circulates in online references. Open Library resolves that ISBN to a different book (the Hazor 1990-2009 Iron Age report), so it cannot be used for Meshel 2012.
  • The site is often called a temple. No temple, altar or conventional cult object was found (Hadley 1993; Schmidt 2016, quoted in Smoak and Schniedewind 2019), and the 2015 Hebrew edition dropped the 'religious site' label.
  • Calling the site 'Judahite' ignores the northern evidence: -yaw names, 'YHWH of Samaria', and pottery with northern ties. Singer-Avitz (2006) is the main dissent, reading the pottery as mostly Judahite.

The Find

  • DateLate 9th to first half of 8th century BCE. Radiocarbon places construction at c. 820-795 BCE and abandonment after 745 BCE; the date is disputed on pottery grounds.
  • How datedRadiocarbon determinations re-analysed by Finkelstein and Piasetzky (Tel Aviv 35, 2008), plus ceramic comparison (Ayalon 1995; Freud 2008 against Singer-Avitz 2006, 2009). The inscriptions are dated with the site and by palaeography.
  • Discovered1975-1976, Ze'ev Meshel (Tel Aviv University); inscriptions published by Shmuel Ahituv, Esther Eshel and Meshel, Kuntillet 'Ajrud, on the Darb el-Ghazza route in northeastern Sinai (Egypt)
  • Now heldSite in northeastern Sinai. The present repository of the pithoi and plaster fragments is not stated in the sources cited here.
  • MaterialInk on two large storage jars (pithoi A and B); ink on wall plaster; incised inscriptions on storage jars and stone vessels
  • ScriptHebrew in a Phoenician-type script (Zevit 2001: 377-378, cited by Smoak and Schniedewind 2019, calls 'Phoenician' anachronistic here)
  • LanguageHebrew with northern (Israelite) features; theophoric names in -yaw

Biblical Passages

  • Deut 16:21-22Forbids planting an asherah, 'any tree', beside YHWH's altar and setting up a standing stone. Kuntillet 'Ajrud pairs YHWH with 'his asherah' in blessings, the practice this law bans. The exact nature of the asherah is the open question above.
  • 1 Kgs 16:33; 2 Kgs 13:6; 21:7; 23:4-7Asherah in Samaria and later in Jerusalem, condemned by the Kings historian. The 'YHWH of Samaria and his asherah' formula is a firsthand northern example from the Jehu dynasty era.
  • Hab 3:3; Deut 33:2; Judg 5:4-5God comes 'from Teman' or from Seir and Sinai, and the mountains quake or melt. The inscriptions have 'YHWH of Teman', and the plaster text has mountains melting when El shines forth.
  • Num 6:24-26'The LORD bless you and keep you'. KA 3.6 has 'may he bless you and may he guard you' in a letter formula, the same pair of verbs.
