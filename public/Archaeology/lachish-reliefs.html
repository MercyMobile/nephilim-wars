Lachish Reliefs

Assyrian palace reliefs of Sennacherib's capture of Lachish (701 BCE) · carved c. 700-681 BCE

Overview

A continuous series of carved wall panels from a single room in Sennacherib's palace at Nineveh, showing the Assyrian assault on the Judahite city of Lachish, the surrender of its people, and the king receiving the spoil and captives. Layard found them late in his second expedition and described them in 1853: thirteen slabs, some almost entire though cracked by fire, with the epigraph 'complete'.

The scenes show a hilly country of figs and vines, a city with double walls and towers, ten ramps built against the walls, seven battering rams, massed archers and slingers, defenders throwing torches, impaled prisoners, families leaving with carts, and Sennacherib enthroned before a tent while prisoners and spoil pass before him.

A short cuneiform caption above the king names Sennacherib and Lachish. The same campaign is described in Sennacherib's annals and in 2 Kings 18-19; the Assyrian siege ramp was later excavated at Tel Lachish itself (Ussishkin).

In its own words

Sennacherib, king of the universe, king of Assyria, sat upon a house chair (while) the booty of Lachish passed before him.D. D. Luckenbill, Ancient Records of Assyria and Babylonia, vol. II (Chicago, 1927), §489, p. 198 (epigraph; after Paterson, The Palace of Sennacherib, pls. 74-76)

What the evidence shows

  • 13 carved slabs from one room (Room XXXVI, 38 x 18 ft) of the South-West Palace at Nineveh (Layard 1853, pp. 148-151).
  • Epigraph above the enthroned king naming Sennacherib and Lachish (Layard 1853, p. 152; Luckenbill 1927, §489).
  • City shown with double walls, towers and outworks; ten ramps and seven battering rams against it (Layard 1853, p. 149).
  • Defenders wear a pointed helmet with a fringed lappet over the ears; some captives wear a turban with a hanging end (Layard 1853, p. 149).
  • Captives and spoil: furniture, arms, shields, chariots, metal vessels, camels, ox-carts carrying women and children; prisoners impaled below the walls (Layard 1853, p. 149).
  • The series ends with a plan of the Assyrian camp with tents, a fire-altar and two chariots bearing standards (Layard 1853, p. 151).
  • British Museum dates the South-West Palace panels to 700-681 BCE and displays them in Room 10b with a prism of Sennacherib's campaign account.

Open questions

  • What exactly does the epigraph say? Layard (1853, p. 152), using the young decipherment of his day, translated: 'Sennacherib, the mighty king, king of the country of Assyria, sitting on the throne of judgment, before (or at the entrance of) the city of Lachish (Lakhisha). I give permission for its slaughter.' Luckenbill (1927, §489), working from Paterson's plates, reads: 'sat upon a house chair (while) the booty of Lachish passed before him'; Barton (1917, p. 373, from Winckler 1892) likewise has 'sat on his throne, and the spoil of the city of Lachish passed before him'. The later readings contain no permission to slaughter; the 1853 wording should not be quoted as the text.
  • Is it the biblical Lachish? Henry Rawlinson (Outlines of Assyrian History, p. xxxvi) placed the caption's city on the coast between Gaza and Rhinocolura; Layard (1853, p. 153 n.), 'supported ... by Dr. Hincks', rejected this and placed it among the cities of Judah listed in Josh 15:39. The epigraph's Lakisu and the excavated siege remains at Tel Lachish (Ussishkin, Journal for Semitics 24/2, 2017) now tie the reliefs to Tel Lachish.
  • How literal is the picture? Ussishkin (2017) treats the reliefs as one of five complementary sources for the siege, alongside the Bible, the annals, the excavated city level and the battle remains; comparing the carved city with the excavated site is his method. Where the relief and the ground disagree in detail is argued in his work; the full article is not summarized here.

The Find

  • DateEvent: Sennacherib's 701 BCE campaign in Judah. Carving: for the South-West Palace at Nineveh, c. 700-681 BCE
  • How datedThe epigraph names Sennacherib and Lachish; the 701 BCE campaign date comes from Sennacherib's annals (third campaign). The reliefs decorate his South-West Palace, dated by the British Museum to 700-681 BCE (gallery text gives 700-692 BCE for Room 10b).
  • DiscoveredDuring Layard's second expedition to Nineveh (published 1853), Austen Henry Layard, Room XXXVI of Sennacherib's South-West Palace, Kuyunjik (Nineveh), near Mosul, Iraq
  • Now heldBritish Museum, London, Room 10b ('Assyria: Siege of Lachish')
  • MaterialGypsum (alabaster) wall slabs carved in low relief
  • DimensionsSeries of 13 slabs lining a room Layard measured as 38 by 18 feet
  • ScriptNeo-Assyrian cuneiform (epigraph above the king)
  • LanguageAkkadian (Assyrian)

Biblical Passages

  • 2 Kgs 18:13-14, 17; 19:8Sennacherib is 'at Lachish' when Hezekiah sends tribute and when the Rabshakeh is sent to Jerusalem; the reliefs show Sennacherib enthroned at Lachish receiving its spoil.
  • 2 Chr 32:9Sennacherib 'himself laid siege against Lachish, and all his power with him' - the reliefs show the massed army and the king present.
  • Isa 36:1-2The Rabshakeh is sent 'from Lachish to Jerusalem' - the same setting.
  • Mic 1:13'O thou inhabitant of Lachish, bind the chariot to the swift beast' - a prophetic line naming Lachish in this era; our reading of its relevance.
