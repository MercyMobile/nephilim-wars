Politarch Inscription (Vardar Gate, Thessalonica)

Greek list of Thessalonica's civic officials, headed by six politarchs · Roman period, undated

Overview

A marble block from the Roman arch that stood over the Via Egnatia at the western entrance of Thessalonica, known locally as the Vardar Gate. Travellers copied the text from 1740 onward; in 1866 a photograph was taken of it in place. When the arch was torn down in 1876, Rev. Peter Crosbie saved the main block; it went to the British consulate and then to the British Museum. A second, narrow block with five more letters was carried off with the rubble and lost in the construction of the harbour quay (acting consul Barker's letter of 1877, quoted by Burton 1898).

The text is a list of officials. It opens with the word ΠΟΛΕΙΤΑΡΧΟΥΝΤΩΝ ('while … were politarchs'), then names six politarchs, the city treasurer (tamias tēs poleōs) and the gymnasiarch. It is not a dedication of a statue, and it does not name an emperor.

Its value for the New Testament is the title. Acts 17:6 and 17:8 call the magistrates of Thessalonica politarchai, a word that is almost absent from literary Greek; this stone and others from Thessalonica show it was the city's real official title.

What the text says (summary)

ΠΟΛΕΙΤΑΡΧΟΥΝΤΩΝ ΣΩΣΙΠΑΤΡΟΥ ΤΟΥ ΚΛΕΟΠΑΤΡΑΣ ΚΑΙ ΛΟΥΚΙΟΥ ΠΟΝΤΙΟΥ ΣΕΚΟΥΝΔΟΥ ΥΙΟΥ … ΤΑΜΙΟΥ ΤΗΣ ΠΟΛΕΩΣ … ΓΥΜΝΑΣΙΑΡΧΟΥΝΤΟΣ … — 'While politarchs were Sosipatros son of Kleopatra and Lucius Pontius Secundus, son …; [five more names]; … treasurer of the city …; … being gymnasiarch …'Literal translation (this encyclopedia) of the Greek text printed by E. D. Burton, 'The Politarchs', American Journal of Theology 2 (1898) p. 600

What the evidence shows

  • The first word is the participle ΠΟΛΕΙΤΑΡΧΟΥΝΤΩΝ, 'being politarchs', spelled with ΕΙ (Burton 1898, 600).
  • Six politarchs are listed, then a treasurer of the city and a gymnasiarch (Burton 1898, 600–602; Boeckh's corrigenda).
  • Some of the names are Roman (Lucius Pontius Secundus, Aulus Avius Sabinus, Gaius Agilleius Potitus) and some Greek (Sosipatros, Demetrios, Zoilos, Tauros).
  • The text carries no date formula.
  • The stone was set in the inside face of an arch on the Via Egnatia, the road Paul would have used between Philippi and Thessalonica (Acts 17:1).

Open questions

  • Date: Boeckh (CIG 1967, 1833) put it no earlier than Vespasian because he read a 'Flavius Sabinus' in line 3. Burton (AJT 1898, 601–602) shows that reading came from bad early copies (Bimardus, Pococke), not the stone, and argues from the count of politarchs (five under Augustus per the Kalamari Gate text; six in the dated 143 CE text) that it is later than 30 BCE and may be as late as 143 CE. The British Museum dates it to the 2nd century CE; the basis for that date is not confirmed in a published museum record available here.
  • How many politarchs in Paul's day? Burton (1898, 627–628) collects a Thessalonica text dated to Augustus' reign with five, and a text of 46 CE with two politarchs that he argues does not come from Thessalonica (it was found in a private courtyard and names no city). Acts gives no number.

Corrected from the earlier edition of this page
  • Old entry: 'discovered in 1876 during excavations'. The inscription was never excavated. It stood in a standing arch and was copied from 1740 (Muratori's publication of Bimardus' copy). 1876 is the year the arch was demolished and the stone removed (Burton 1898, 600, 603).
  • Old text 'The politarchs of the city erected a statue of the emperor' is invented. The stone is a list of officials, six politarchs plus a treasurer and gymnasiarch. It mentions no statue and no emperor (Burton 1898, 600).
  • Old location 'British Museum, Greece' is wrong. It is in the British Museum in London.
  • Old date 'c. 50 CE' has no basis in the text. The stone is undated; proposals run from after 30 BCE to the 2nd century CE.
  • Old dimensions '20 cm × 30 cm' are unsourced, and no measurement could be verified. They are dropped.
  • Old biblical links: Acts 17:34 is Dionysius the Areopagite and Damaris in Athens, not a 'Council of politarchs'. Acts 17:32 is the Athenian reaction to the resurrection and has nothing to do with Thessalonica. The only verses that use the title are Acts 17:6 and 17:8.
  • Old claim 'Confirms Luke's terminology' is true in a narrow sense. The title is real and belongs to Thessalonica; this stone may be later than Paul's visit, and the Augustan-era Kalamari Gate inscription (now lost) is the earlier Thessalonica witness (Burton 1898, 604–607).

The Find

  • DateUndated in its text. Burton (1898) places it after 30 BCE and possibly as late as 143 CE or later; the British Museum and later writers give 2nd century CE.
  • How datedNo date formula survives. Boeckh's Flavian date rested on a misread name (a 'Flavius Sabinus' that is not on the stone). Burton argued from the number of politarchs: five in an Augustan-era Thessalonica inscription, six in one dated 143 CE, six here.
  • DiscoveredKnown and copied by 1740 (Bimardus, published by Muratori); removed 1876, Stone rescued by Rev. Peter Crosbie with British consul Blunt when the arch was demolished in 1876, Inside face of the Roman arch called the Vardar Gate, on the Via Egnatia at the western entrance to Thessalonica

Biblical Passages

  • Acts 17:6The Jews of Thessalonica drag Jason and some believers 'before the politarchs' (epi tous politarchas). The stone uses the same office title for the city's magistrates.
  • Acts 17:8'They troubled the crowd and the politarchs.' Same title.
  • Acts 17:5The mob seeks to bring Paul before the dēmos (the people's assembly). Burton notes that a dated Thessalonica inscription of 143 CE also names the dēmos and boulē, a separate point from this stone.
