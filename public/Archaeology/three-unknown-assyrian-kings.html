Three previously unknown Assyrian kings (Edmonds and Frahm 2026)

Kings erased from the Assyrian King List, reconstructed from a grant, a ritual text and a recut stela · 10th-8th century BCE

Overview

In Journal of Cuneiform Studies 78 (2026) Alexander Edmonds and Eckart Frahm argue that three men ruled Assyria briefly but were left out of the Assyrian King List (AKL). No new excavation is involved; the case is built from long-known texts and objects, read together with the Eponym Chronicle.

The three: (1) an Assur-uballit, c. 913-912 BCE, named in a text (VAT 10136) that lists rulers who restored a silver ritual vessel for beer dedicated to the god Ashur; (2) a Tiglath-pileser, c. 763-762 BCE, in whose name a royal land grant (British Museum Rm 75) is dated to 762 BCE, during the years the Eponym Chronicle records 'rebellion in the Inner City (Ashur)'; (3) a Shalmaneser, c. 747-745 BCE, whose name, the authors argue, is the one erased on the Tell Abta stela of the palace herald Bel-Harran-belu-usur and replaced by the name of Tiglath-pileser III.

The Tell Abta stela is the most concrete piece. Its line 9 now reads 'Bel-Harran-belu-usur, the palace herald of Tiglath-pileser, king of Assyria', with the royal name cut over an erasure. Since Unger (1917) the erased name has been read as Shalmaneser IV (782-773 BCE). Edmonds and Frahm assign it instead to a later, unlisted Shalmaneser just before Tiglath-pileser III, citing among other things the official's robe, which BAS reports they date to the mid-8th century.

What the evidence shows

  • Tell Abta stela, Ist 01326, line 9: 'Bel-Harran-belu-usur, the palace herald of Tiglath-pileser, king of Assyria'; the signs of the royal name are marked damaged in the ORACC transliteration, and the king's name is cut over an erased Shalmaneser (ORACC RIAo Shalmaneser IV 2; Luckenbill 1926 §824).
  • The stela's upper part carries divine symbols and a standing human figure in profile, most likely the herald himself (ORACC RIAo introduction).
  • In the stela the herald, not the king, founds the city of Dur-Bel-Harran-belu-usur, builds its temple and grants it freedom from grain and straw taxes and corvée (ORACC translation lines 9-22; Luckenbill §§824-826).
  • Eponym Chronicle 763 BCE (Bur-Saggile): 'rebellion in the Inner City; in the month of Simanu an eclipse of the sun took place'; 762 BCE: 'rebellion in the Inner City' (ORACC SAAo Q007771).
  • Eponym Chronicle 746 BCE: 'rebellion in Kalhu'; 745 BCE: 'in the month of Ayyaru, [day] 13, Tiglath-pileser took the throne' (ORACC SAAo Q007771).
  • Eponym Chronicle 741 BCE lists Bel-Harran-belu-usur, palace herald, as eponym; ORACC notes he had been replaced as herald by 751 and reinstated under Tiglath-pileser III.
  • Rm 75 obverse: fragment of a royal land grant dated 762 BCE issued in the name of a Tiglath-pileser, king of Assyria (Yale News caption). The authors note the grant 'was likely not made in the name of Adad-nerari III, as the name of that king is written differently in other texts' (Yale News).
  • VAT 10136: a text listing rulers who restored a silver beer vessel for Ashur, naming an Assur-uballit with implied royal status (Yale News; BAS).

Open questions

  • Whose name was erased on the Tell Abta stela. Unger (1917, as reported by Luckenbill 1926 §824 n.) and the RIMA 3 / ORACC edition (A.0.105.2, filed under Shalmaneser IV, 782-773 BCE) read it as Shalmaneser IV, with Bel-Harran-belu-usur serving him first and Tiglath-pileser III later. Edmonds and Frahm (JCS 78, 2026) read it as an otherwise unknown Shalmaneser of c. 747-745 BCE; BAS reports their evidence includes the figure's 'tartan' robe, which they date to the mid-8th century. The eponym entries (herald removed by 751, reinstated 741) are compatible with either reading; the robe dating is the new element. The JCS text has not been reviewed for this entry.
  • Whether a single dated grant makes a king. The Tiglath-pileser of 763-762 rests on Rm 75 plus the Eponym Chronicle's two years of 'rebellion in the Inner City'. The chronicle entries do not name the rebel. The case is cumulative ('textual, chronological, archaeological, and artistic evidence', BAS); what else the authors bring was not seen here.
  • The Assur-uballit of c. 913-912 depends on VAT 10136 naming him in a list of restorers of a cult vessel; whether that list implies he was king (BAS: 'implied royal status') or a crown prince is the point to test against the JCS text.

Corrected from the earlier edition of this page
  • Headlines saying three kings were 'discovered' or 'uncovered' imply new finds. The evidence is long-known: the Tell Abta stela was found in 1894 and published by Scheil (1894) and Unger (1917), with the recut royal name already noted by Unger (Luckenbill 1926 §§823-824; ORACC RIAo). What is new is the reading of the erased name as a separate Shalmaneser of 747-745 and the assembling of the three cases.
  • The erasure on the Tell Abta stela is not a new observation: the standard edition already reads a Shalmaneser under Tiglath-pileser III's name, assigning it to Shalmaneser IV (782-773). The dispute is which Shalmaneser, not whether one was erased.
  • The Shalmaneser proposed for 747-745 BCE is not the biblical Shalmaneser of 2 Kgs 17:3 and 18:9 (Shalmaneser V, 727-722 BCE, who took the throne after Tiglath-pileser III per the Eponym Chronicle entry for 727).

The Find

  • DateProposed reigns: Assur-uballit c. 913-912 BCE; Tiglath-pileser c. 763-762 BCE; Shalmaneser c. 747-745 BCE (Edmonds and Frahm 2026)
  • How datedAbsolute dates hang on the Assyrian Eponym Chronicle, anchored by the solar eclipse it records in the eponymy of Bur-Saggile (763 BCE; identified with the eclipse of 15 June 763 BCE in the ORACC edition notes). The Rm 75 grant is dated by eponym to 762 BCE (Yale report). The other two reigns are placed by the authors from the political context; the specific reasoning was not read in the JCS article itself.
  • DiscoveredArgument published June 2026 (JCS 78); the objects themselves are old finds: the Tell Abta stela was found in 1894; Rm 75 is a British Museum tablet; VAT 10136 is a Berlin tablet, Alexander Johannes Edmonds (University of Münster) and Eckart Frahm (Yale University), Tell Abta (Dur-Bel-Harran-belu-usur) on the Wadi Tharthar for the stela; Nineveh for Rm 75 (per BAS); VAT 10136 find-spot not given in the sources read
  • Now heldTell Abta stela: Istanbul Archaeological Museums, Ist 01326 (ORACC RIAo). Rm 75: British Museum (Yale News). VAT 10136: Vorderasiatisches Museum, Berlin (by the VAT prefix; no museum record seen).
  • MaterialTell Abta stela: stone (ORACC catalogue); 'alabaster' per Luckenbill 1926. Rm 75 and VAT 10136: clay tablets
  • ScriptNeo-Assyrian cuneiform
  • LanguageAkkadian

Biblical Passages

  • 2 Kings 15:19-20, 29; 16:7-10; 1 Chronicles 5:26Pul / Tiglath-pileser III, who took Menahem's tribute and later deported Galileans and Transjordanians. The Tell Abta stela shows his name cut in over a predecessor's; if Edmonds and Frahm are right, a Shalmaneser held the throne in the two years before Tiglath-pileser's accession (Ayyaru 745 BCE). It does not change the date of that accession, which is fixed by the Eponym Chronicle.
  • 2 Kings 17:3; 18:9Shalmaneser king of Assyria who besieged Samaria is Shalmaneser V (727-722 BCE), the son of Tiglath-pileser III. The proposed Shalmaneser of 747-745 is a different, earlier man; the two should not be confused.
  • 2 Kings 14:23-29; 15:8-16Israel under Jeroboam II, Zechariah and Shallum (mid-8th century) coincides with the Assyrian rebellions of 763-759 and 746 in the Eponym Chronicle. The Bible does not mention Assyrian interventions in those years; this is a chronological overlap only.
