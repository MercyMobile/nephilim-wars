Nebuchadnezzar II's Palace Inscriptions

Royal building texts of Nebuchadnezzar II describing his palaces at Babylon · 604-562 BCE

Overview

Nebuchadnezzar II left hundreds of building inscriptions - on cylinders, stone tablets and stamped bricks - but almost none report his wars. The largest is the East India House Inscription, a ten-column stone tablet found at Babylon before 1803, which recounts his work on the temples of Esagila and Ezida, the city walls and gates, and above all the rebuilding of the old palace and the building of new palaces 'between the walls' and to the north.

Langdon (1905) showed that the East India House text is an enlarged re-edition of an earlier palace cylinder known in two copies (British Museum 85-4-30,1 and Louvre AO 1506). The palace passages match what Koldewey excavated on the Kasr: a palace raised on a high platform next to his father Nabopolassar's, with thick walls of burnt brick in bitumen and lime mortar.

None of these texts mention Judah or Jerusalem. Barton (1917) noted this directly: 'His inscriptions give no account of these events.' Their biblical interest is the king's own boast of building Babylon, compared with Daniel 4:30. In 2025 two new Nebuchadnezzar cylinders from the ziggurat of Kish and a full edition of 527 administrative tablets from the Babylon palace archives were published.

In its own words

Upon the top of it a great abode for my royal dwelling place with mortar and burnt brick I made high. With the palace of my father I annexed it. In a favorable month, upon a lucky day, I placed its foundation upon the breast of the abyss. Its top I raised like a rocky cliff. In 15 days its work I completed.East India House Inscription col. VIII 59 - IX 1, trans. S. Langdon, Building Inscriptions of the Neo-Babylonian Empire I (1905), p. 139

What the evidence shows

  • EIH: ten columns, 624 lines, archaizing script; first published in London in 1803 (Langdon 1905).
  • EIH describes restoring Nabopolassar's old palace, whose foundations had been weakened by flood water and whose gates had sunk below the raised street (Langdon 1905, p. 135).
  • EIH describes a new palace built between the city walls, joined to his father's palace, 'In 15 days its work I completed' (Langdon 1905, p. 139).
  • EIH describes rebuilding the gates of Imgur-Bel and Nimitti-Bel with bulls and serpents and bronze bulls at their thresholds (Langdon 1905, p. 131) - the Ishtar Gate program.
  • Koldewey's excavation of the Kasr (1899-1917) found Nebuchadnezzar's palace on a raised platform, with lime mortar used in his latest buildings (Kasr, Principal Citadel, Babil) (Koldewey 1914, p. 31).
  • Barton's selection of Nebuchadnezzar texts (1917) contains a general claim of conquests 'from the upper sea to the lower sea', the Wadi Brisa (Lebanon) text, and a wall-building text; none mentions Judah (Barton 1917, pp. 379-380).
  • Pedersén (2025) publishes 527 administrative texts from the South Palace archives of Nebuchadnezzar's reign; its abstract states the largest single oil allotment went to 'the biblical king Jehoiachin'.

Open questions

  • Why no Judah? The royal building inscriptions describe buildings and piety; Nebuchadnezzar's 597 BCE capture of Jerusalem is known instead from a separate genre of text, the Babylonian chronicles. Barton (1917, p. 380) states that his inscriptions 'give no account of these events'. Whether this silence reflects genre or choice is a reading, not something the texts say.
  • Which copy is the 'real' text? Langdon (1905) argued the Louvre cylinder (AO 1506, his 'B') is the earlier original and the British Museum cylinder ('A') a later, Marduk-focused copy, with EIH an enlargement of B. That textual history is Langdon's reconstruction from comparing wording (e.g. 'their lordship' vs 'his lordship'); later editions have not been checked against it for this entry.
  • The '15 days' claim for the new palace is the text's own figure; nothing in the excavation report tests it.

The Find

  • DateReign of Nebuchadnezzar II, 604-562 BCE; the East India House Inscription belongs to the later part of the reign (Langdon 1905 groups it with the palace-building texts after c. 590)
  • How datedThe texts name Nebuchadnezzar son of Nabopolassar; relative order from their contents (which buildings are described as finished) as worked out by Langdon (1905).
  • DiscoveredEast India House Inscription: found before 1803 in the ruins of Babylon and published in London in 1803; palace buildings excavated by the Deutsche Orient-Gesellschaft 1899-1917, EIH obtained by Sir Harford Jones Brydges (British Resident in Baghdad); palace excavated by Robert Koldewey, Babylon (Iraq): the Kasr mound (Southern Citadel/palace), with other copies of related texts from Babylon
  • Now heldEast India House Inscription: British Museum, London (registration 1938,0520.1 per secondary listing; the museum's own record was not reachable). Related cylinders: British Museum 85-4-30,1 and Louvre AO 1506 (Langdon 1905).
  • MaterialEIH: dark stone tablet (Langdon 1905: 'black basalt'); related texts on clay cylinders
  • DimensionsEIH: about 56.5 x 50.2 cm (Langdon: 28 inches square, 4 inches thick); ten columns, 624 lines (Langdon 1905)
  • ScriptEIH in archaizing Babylonian cuneiform; cylinders in Neo-Babylonian script
  • LanguageAkkadian (Babylonian)

Biblical Passages

  • Dan 4:29-30'Is not this great Babylon, that I have built for the house of the kingdom by the might of my power, and for the honour of my majesty?' The king's inscriptions make exactly this kind of claim about building the palace and the city (e.g. EIH: 'a great abode for my royal dwelling place ... I made high'). The texts do not mention a madness or exile of the king.
  • 2 Kgs 24-25; Jer 52Nebuchadnezzar is the conqueror of Jerusalem in the biblical record; these building inscriptions do not mention the campaign (Barton 1917).
  • 2 Kgs 25:27-30; Jer 52:31-34The palace's administrative archive (Pedersén 2025) includes the oil lists naming Jehoiachin; see jehoiachin-rations-tablets.
