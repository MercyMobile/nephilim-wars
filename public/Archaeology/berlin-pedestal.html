Berlin Pedestal Relief

Granite statue-base fragment with three bound Asiatic captives and name rings · Egyptian, date disputed

Overview

A slab of grey granite, probably from the base of a royal statue. It shows three bound Western Asiatic captives, each with an oval 'name ring' on his body naming a conquered place, and above them a band of hieroglyphs reading '[...] one who is falling on his feet'. Ludwig Borchardt bought it for Berlin in 1913 from the dealer M. Nachman, together with a companion slab (ÄM 21688) of about the same size, which shows two Asiatic captives and one Nubian captive.

The left name ring reads ʿ-s-q-l-n, Ashkelon. The middle reads k-y-n-ʿ-nw, Canaan. The right ring is damaged, apparently by modern hacking. It reads i-[?]-šȝ-i-r, and the second sign is almost entirely lost. In 2001 Manfred Görg restored the missing sign as a vulture (ȝ). He read the whole as an archaic writing of 'Israel' and argued that the three names mirror Merenptah's stele (Canaan, Ashkelon, ... Israel). In 2010 P. van der Veen, C. Theis and Görg published photographs taken under raking light and an aluminium-foil impression. They say these show the vulture's leg and claw.

The reading matters because if the third ring does name Israel in the 18th Dynasty (or in a list copied from one), it would put an entity called Israel in Canaan before Merenptah's Year 5 (c. 1208 BCE). J. K. Hoffmeier rejected the reading on the grounds of its sibilant and its orthography. The stone itself cannot settle the question, because its key sign is damaged.

What the text says (summary)

Left ring: ʿ-s-q-l-n (Ashkelon). Middle ring: k-y-n-ʿ-nw (Canaan). Right ring: i-[ȝ?]-šȝ-i-r, damaged, restored by Görg as 'Israel'. Band above the captives: '[...] ḫtb.w ḥr rd.wi=f', 'one who is falling on his feet...'.Transliterations and the band translation from P. van der Veen, C. Theis & M. Görg, JAEI 2:4 (2010), pp. 15-17. No public-domain translation exists.

What the evidence shows

  • Grey granite slab, 46 × 39.5 cm, sawn to 7.5 cm thick, bearing three bound Asiatic captives with name rings (van der Veen et al. 2010, p. 15).
  • Bought in 1913 by Borchardt from M. Nachman, with no findspot (museum archival notes as reported in van der Veen et al. 2010). The companion slab ÄM 21688 (50 × 38 cm, also grey granite) shows two Asiatic captives and one Nubian captive and names Retenu.
  • Ring 1, Ashkelon, is written consonantally without group-writing. The closest parallel is pLeningrad 1116A from the reign of Amenhotep II.
  • Ring 2, Canaan (k-y-n-ʿ-nw), has close parallels in Amenhotep II's Memphis and Karnak stelae. It differs from the usual Ramesside form pȝ-Knʿn.
  • Ring 3 is damaged along its right edge, probably by modern hacking. The first sign is the reed-leaf (i). The second is almost lost; van der Veen and Theis report traces of a vulture's leg and claw from raking-light photography and a foil impression. The remaining signs are the pool-sign šȝ, the reed-leaf i, and r.
  • Van der Veen et al. report that measuring the ring shows no room for any further sign after šȝ, i and r.
  • No king's name survives on the fragment.

Open questions

  • Does ring 3 read 'Israel'? M. Görg (Biblische Notizen 106, 2001, 21-27) restored the second sign as a vulture (ȝ). He read the name as iȝ-šr-il / yȝ-šr-il, taking the ȝ of šȝ as r, as in Middle Kingdom spellings such as Rw-šȝ-mm for Jerusalem in the Execration Texts. Van der Veen, Theis & Görg (JAEI 2:4, 2010) say that raking-light photographs and a foil impression show the vulture's leg and claw. J. K. Hoffmeier (JETS 50/2, 2007, 241-242) answers that the name is 'plagued by serious linguistic and orthographic problems'. His points: Israel needs ś, not š (Merenptah's stele writes it with s); šȝ is a group-writing for šā, as in Sharuhen; a restored ȝ would stand for Semitic lamed; and the final r is not the l-sign used in 'Ashkelon' on the same block. Van der Veen et al. reply that Egyptian scribes varied their signs for foreign names, for example in the Egyptian spelling of Kurigalzu. The dispute turns on one damaged sign and on the rules for rendering sibilants.
  • Date. Hoffmeier (2007, p. 241) reports that Görg dated the block on style to Thutmose III or Amenhotep II. Van der Veen et al. (2010, p. 15) report Görg's tentative 19th-Dynasty date (Ramesses II), based on the resemblance to Merenptah's stele, while granting that the spellings copy an earlier 18th-Dynasty source. R. Giveon (Eretz-Israel 15, 1981, as cited by van der Veen et al.) dated it to the 18th Dynasty. With no provenance or royal name, the date floats with the spellings.
  • Unknown: whether ÄM 21687 and ÄM 21688 came from the same monument, where the statue stood, and which king it portrayed. The sources are silent.

The Find

  • DateDisputed: 18th Dynasty (Giveon; Görg's early view, Thutmose III or Amenhotep II as reported by Hoffmeier 2007) or 19th Dynasty, possibly Ramesses II (Görg 2001 as reported by van der Veen, Theis & Görg 2010), perhaps copied from an older list
  • How datedThe date rests on style and spelling alone. The block has no provenance and no text naming a king. The Ashkelon and Canaan spellings are consonantal and match 18th-Dynasty forms (Amenhotep II). The trio of Ashkelon, Canaan and a third name resembles the sequence on Merenptah's stele.
  • DiscoveredBought 1913, Ludwig Borchardt, from the dealer M. Nachman (per the Berlin museum's archival notes as reported by van der Veen et al. 2010), Unknown. It was bought on the antiquities market and never excavated.
  • Now heldÄgyptisches Museum und Papyrussammlung, Staatliche Museen zu Berlin, inv. ÄM 21687
  • MaterialGrey granite
  • Dimensions46 cm high × 39.5 cm wide; 7.5 cm thick as sawn (probably cut from a larger block in modern times); name rings 18 × 9 cm (van der Veen et al. 2010, p. 15 and n. 7, n. 10)
  • ScriptEgyptian hieroglyphs
  • LanguageEgyptian (the place names are Semitic)

Biblical Passages

  • Merenptah Stele (context); Judg 1:1-2:5If ring 3 is Israel, the block would place a group or land called Israel beside Ashkelon and Canaan in an Egyptian list, possibly two centuries before Merenptah. That would bear on the date of Israel's presence in Canaan implied by the settlement accounts.
  • 1 Kgs 6:1Advocates of a 15th-century Exodus (B. G. Wood, JETS 48, 2005) cite the block as support for Israel in Canaan under the 18th Dynasty, and so for the 480-year figure. That depends on both the 'Israel' reading and the 18th-Dynasty date, and both are disputed.
  • Josh 13:3; Judg 1:18Ashkelon appears in the same ring sequence. The biblical texts place Ashkelon among the Philistine and Canaanite coastal cities, and here it stands beside Canaan.
