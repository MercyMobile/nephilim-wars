Amarna Letters

Cuneiform diplomatic archive of Amenhotep III and Akhenaten, with letters from Jerusalem's ruler · 14th century BCE

Overview

In 1887-88 villagers at Tell el-Amarna found clay tablets inscribed in cuneiform. They turned out to be the foreign correspondence of Amenhotep III and Akhenaten. Barton (1916, p. 35) describes 'nearly four hundred' tablets. The archive includes letters from the great kings of Babylon, Assyria, Mitanni and Hatti, and from vassal rulers in Canaan and Phoenicia: Rib-Adda of Byblos, Lab'ayu of Shechem, and the rulers of Gezer, Lachish, Megiddo and others.

Six letters (EA 285-290) come from Abdi-Heba (older reading Ebed-Hepa), ruler of Urusalim, Jerusalem. He protests his loyalty, complains that the pharaoh's officials slander him, and begs for archers. He warns that the 'Habiru/'Apiru' are plundering the king's lands and that towns such as Gezer, Ashkelon and Lachish are aiding his enemies. His letters mention Keilah and Aijalon, which also appear in Joshua.

A complete re-edition based on collation of every extant tablet was published by A. F. Rainey (ed. W. M. Schniedewind and Z. Cochavi-Rainey, Brill 2015). A new translation by J. Lauinger and T. Yoder appeared in 2025.

In its own words

There are no lands left to the king, my lord. The Habiri plunder all the countries of the king. If there are mercenaries in this year, then there will be left countries of the king, my lord. If there are no mercenaries, the countries of the king will be lost.Ebed-Hepa (Abdi-Heba) of Jerusalem, EA 286 (Knudtzon 286 = Winckler-Abel 102), trans. G. A. Barton, Archaeology and the Bible (Philadelphia, 1916), Part II ch. XV, p. 346

What the evidence shows

  • Found 1887-88 at Tell el-Amarna; Barton (1916, p. 35) gives 'nearly four hundred' tablets 'inscribed in the Babylonian language and characters'.
  • EA 285-290 each open 'um-ma ARAD-hi/he-ba', 'thus Abdi-Heba' (CDLI transliterations checked for all six); CDLI places all six in the Vorderasiatisches Museum, Berlin (VAT 1601, 1642, 1644, 1643, 1645+2709, 1646).
  • EA 286 opens (CDLI transliteration): a-na LUGAL EN-ia qi-bi-ma um-ma ARAD-he-ba ARAD-ka-ma, 'To the king my lord, say: thus Abdi-Heba your servant'.
  • In EA 286 Abdi-Heba says neither his father nor his mother set him in his place but 'the arm of the mighty king' (Barton 1916 p. 346).
  • In EA 287 Abdi-Heba names Gezer, Ashkelon and Lachish as having given food and oil (to his enemies), the 'sons of Labaya' as giving the land to the Habiri, and 'the land of the city of Jerusalem' as granted to him by the king (Barton p. 346-347).
  • EA 287 mentions a caravan captured 'in the fields near the city Aijalon' and a matter involving Keilah (Barton p. 346-347).
  • Rainey's 2015 edition is based on collation of all extant tablets (Brill, HdO 110).

Open questions

  • Are the Habiru the Hebrews? Barton (1916, Part I p. 188) held that the Habiri 'may have been the first wave of the Hebrew conquest', and elsewhere that they 'were probably the Hebrews'. In the letters themselves the Habiru are a force that local rulers can side with: Abdi-Heba accuses Malkiel and the sons of Labaya of having 'given the land of the king to the Habiri' (EA 287, Barton p. 347). That is, the texts describe the Habiru by what they do, not by descent. The linguistic link 'apiru / 'ibri and the social-category reading were not settled from the primary sources cited here.
  • When relative to Joshua? Barton (1916, Part II p. 344) records that some readers took the letters as patriarchal-age and others as contemporary with the conquest. Which of these holds depends on the date assigned to the Exodus and conquest (1 Kgs 6:1, Judg 11:26 versus Exod 1:11). The letters themselves name no Israelite leader or tribe.
  • How big was Abdi-Heba's Jerusalem? Barton (1916, p. 187-188) reads the letters as describing a walled city and a sizeable kingdom reaching Keilah and Aijalon. The excavated Late Bronze remains on the Southeastern Ridge are sparse. The texts show a ruler with a scribe and diplomatic reach; they do not measure the town.

Corrected from the earlier edition of this page
  • Claims that the letters prove the Habiru were the Israelites: the letters say the Habiru plunder the king's lands and that local rulers gave land to them. Identifying them with the Hebrews is a reading. Barton (1916) himself wrote only 'may have been' and 'probably'.
  • Claims that the Abdi-Heba letters are in the British Museum or Cairo: CDLI records put EA 285-290 in the Vorderasiatisches Museum, Berlin.
  • Count of Abdi-Heba letters: Barton (1916, p. 35) says seven. The CDLI transliterations show six letters opening 'thus Abdi-Heba' (EA 285-290); the seventh in older counts is not identified in the sources cited here.
  • Claims that the letters 'confirm the conquest narrative': they name no Israelite person or tribe. They show Canaanite city-states such as Jerusalem, Shechem, Gezer and Lachish under Egyptian overlordship, complaining of Habiru raids.

The Find

  • DateReigns of Amenhotep III and Amenhotep IV / Akhenaten, 14th century BCE (Barton 1916: Abdi-Heba's letters c. 1375-1357 BCE)
  • How datedThe letters are addressed to named pharaohs (Amenophis III and IV in Barton's terms) and were found at Akhetaten, the city Akhenaten founded and which was abandoned soon after his reign. BCE figures depend on Egyptian chronology.
  • DiscoveredWinter 1887-1888, Local villagers (Barton 1916 repeats the account of 'a native Egyptian woman'); tablets then dispersed through the antiquities market, Tell el-Amarna (ancient Akhetaten), east bank of the Nile, Middle Egypt
  • Now heldDispersed. Abdi-Heba's Jerusalem letters EA 285-290 are in the Vorderasiatisches Museum, Berlin (EA 285 = VAT 1601; EA 286 = VAT 1642; EA 287 = VAT 1644; EA 288 = VAT 1643; EA 289 = VAT 1645 + VAT 2709; EA 290 = VAT 1646). Others are in the British Museum (e.g., EA 252 = BM 29844), Cairo and elsewhere.
  • MaterialClay tablets
  • ScriptCuneiform
  • LanguageAkkadian (the diplomatic language), with heavy Canaanite features in letters from the Levant

Biblical Passages

  • Josh 10:1-5; 15:63; Judg 1:21Jerusalem is a city-state with its own king before Israel holds it. In the 14th century BCE, EA 285-290 show Urusalim ruled by Abdi-Heba as an Egyptian vassal.
  • Josh 10:12; 15:44; 1 Sam 23:1-13Aijalon and Keilah appear in Abdi-Heba's letters (EA 287 in Barton's translation) as places in Jerusalem's sphere, and in the Bible as towns of the Shephelah and hill-country border.
  • Gen 14:13; Exod 1-3 ('ibri / Hebrews)Some readers equate the Habiru who 'plunder all the countries of the king' with the Hebrews. That equation is a reading, not a statement of the text.
  • Judg 9 (Shechem); Josh 24Lab'ayu and his sons at Shechem (mentioned by Abdi-Heba) show Shechem as a regional power in the 14th century, the city where Joshua 24 and Judges 9 are set.
