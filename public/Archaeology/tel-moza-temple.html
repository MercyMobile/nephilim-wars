Tel Moẓa Temple

Iron Age II temple complex with courtyard altar and cult objects, 7 km northwest of Jerusalem · built in the Iron IIA, late 10th–early 9th century BCE

Overview

Tel Moẓa lies on the main approach to Jerusalem from the west. Salvage excavations ahead of the rebuilding of Highway 1 had already shown an Iron Age settlement that its excavators read as a royal granary supplying Jerusalem. In 2012 new salvage work showed that beneath the late Iron Age 'Building 500' lay a temple complex of the Iron IIA.

The temple faces east. Its entrance has a portico with two column bases set between antae, opening into a main hall. Only the northern part of the building was excavated, and the southern half is restored by symmetry; an inner shrine is suggested by differences in the floor but, in the excavator's words, 'cannot be positively ascertained'. In the courtyard stood a square altar of fieldstones beside a pit filled with ash, sherds and the bones of young animals, and a stone podium around which cult objects lay: figurines of people and horses, chalices and a tall decorated stand. They had been covered deliberately with earth and plaster (Kisilevitz 2015).

Since 2019 the Tel Moẓa Expedition Project has dug the site as a research excavation. It reports a structure beneath the temple and at least four phases in the courtyard, and compares the plan with the temples of Tell Tayinat and 'Ain Dara and with the description of Solomon's temple in 1 Kings 6–7. In 2025 Kisilevitz described the sequence as two temples, a modest early shrine replaced by a monumental long-room temple.

What the evidence shows

  • Location: about 7 km northwest of the City of David (Kisilevitz 2015, p. 147; expedition website).
  • Plan: east-facing building with a portico of two column bases flanked by antae; main hall 7–7.5 m wide; reconstructed size about 18 × 13 m. Excavated were the northern courtyard, the building's northeast corner and part of its west side; the southern half is inferred (Kisilevitz 2015, pp. 150–151).
  • A possible inner shrine is suggested only by floor differences and 'cannot be positively ascertained' (Kisilevitz 2015, p. 151 n. 11).
  • Courtyard: a rectangular fieldstone altar about 1.35 × 1.4 m; beside it a pit about 1.85 × 1.3 m with ash, sherds and many animal bones, preliminarily described as young, unblemished animals (Kisilevitz 2015, pp. 151–154).
  • Cult objects clustered at a stone podium about 1 m north of the pit: two human head figurines, a large hollow horse with the remains of a rider, a smaller solid equid, a tall stand with lion or sphinx reliefs, chalices and a pomegranate-shaped pendant (Kisilevitz 2015, pp. 154–160).
  • The objects were deliberately covered with earth and plaster and sealed beneath later Iron II walls and floors (Kisilevitz 2015, pp. 156, 161).
  • Date of construction: Iron IIA, probably early in the late Iron IIA, no later than the first half of the 9th century BCE (Kisilevitz 2015, p. 149 n. 8).
  • The expedition reports at least four courtyard phases spanning the late 10th/early 9th to the late 7th/early 6th centuries BCE, and a plan of the North Syrian type known at Tell Tayinat and 'Ain Dara (expedition website).
  • A structure beneath the temple, partly uncovered and tentatively dated to the 10th century BCE (Tel Aviv University statement, 4 February 2020).

Open questions

  • Is there an inner room? The 2015 report leaves it open, and the south half of the building was never dug. Descriptions that give the temple a main hall and a holy of holies go beyond what was excavated by 2013.
  • How long was it a temple? Kisilevitz (2015) gives no end date, reports that the cult objects were buried under later Iron II floors, and says it is not possible to tell whether the 7th–early 6th century Building 500 was still a temple. D. R. Moulis (2019) places the covering of the altar and temple in the 8th century BCE. The expedition now reports courtyard phases to the late 7th/early 6th century, and the Biblical Archaeology Society's summary of Kisilevitz and Lipschits (2020) has the temple functional until the early 6th century.
  • Whose temple? The 2015 report names no deity. Kisilevitz and Lipschits (2020, 2022) argue that it began as the work of a local group tied to the granary and came to stand within Judah's official system, so that sanctioned temples existed beside Jerusalem's. That reading rests on the site's economic role and its nearness to the capital; no inscription from Moẓa names the god, the builders or a king.
  • What of Hezekiah and Josiah? 2 Kings credits both kings with removing sanctuaries outside Jerusalem. A temple 7 km from the city with phases into the late 7th century bears directly on how far, and when, those measures reached. The excavators raise the question; the stratigraphy published so far does not settle it.

The Find

  • DateTemple built in the Iron IIA, 'no later than the first half of the 9th century BCE' (Kisilevitz 2015); the expedition gives the late 10th–early 9th century BCE and reports at least four courtyard phases down to the late 7th/early 6th century BCE
  • How datedPottery from stratified floors and fills (Kisilevitz 2015, p. 149 with n. 8). No radiocarbon dates are reported in the sources read here.
  • DiscoveredSalvage excavations 1993 and 2002–2003; the temple recognised in salvage excavations of 2012–2013; Tel Moẓa Expedition Project from spring 2019, Israel Antiquities Authority (Z. Greenhut and A. De Groot; then S. Kisilevitz, A. Eirikh-Rose, H. Khalaily and Z. Greenhut); Tel Moẓa Expedition Project of Tel Aviv University and the IAA (S. Kisilevitz and O. Lipschits), Tel Moẓa, about 7 km northwest of the City of David, on the line of Highway 1
  • Now heldIn situ; the part exposed in 2012–2013 was backfilled in 2013 and lies beneath the highway bridge (Kisilevitz 2015, p. 150 n. 10). Finds with the Israel Antiquities Authority
  • MaterialStone-built temple with portico; fieldstone altar; refuse pit with ash and animal bone; pottery cult stand; clay figurines
  • DimensionsReconstructed building about 18 × 13 m; main hall 7–7.5 m wide inside; altar about 1.35 × 1.4 m (Kisilevitz 2015, pp. 150–151)

Biblical Passages

  • Joshua 18:26Mozah is listed among the towns of Benjamin. The site's name preserves it.
  • 1 Kings 6:2–5; 7:15–21; 2 Chronicles 3A long-room temple entered from the east through a porch with two pillars. The Moẓa portico has two column bases at an east entrance. The excavators compare the two plans, and both belong to a building type also known at Tell Tayinat and 'Ain Dara. The comparison is of type; nothing identifies the Moẓa building with Jerusalem's.
  • 1 Kings 3:2–4; 15:14; 22:43The text itself says the people and kings of Judah went on sacrificing at high places outside Jerusalem. Moẓa is an excavated place of sacrifice in Judah from that period.
  • 2 Kings 18:4, 22; 23:8The reforms of Hezekiah and Josiah against sanctuaries outside Jerusalem. See the open question on the date at which the Moẓa temple went out of use.
