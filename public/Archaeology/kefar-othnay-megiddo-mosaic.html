Kefar 'Othnay Prayer Hall Mosaic (the Megiddo Mosaic)

Mosaic floor of a Christian prayer hall with three Greek inscriptions, one offering a table 'to God Jesus Christ' · 3rd century CE

Overview

In 2005 an excavation inside Megiddo Prison uncovered a room with a mosaic floor in a large building at Kefar 'Othnay, the village beside the camp of the Sixth Legion Ferrata. The floor carries a panel with two fish in a roundel and three Greek inscriptions, and the base of a table stood in it.

One inscription says that Gaianus, also called Porphyrius, a centurion, 'our brother', paid for the mosaic from his own money, and that Brutius made it. A second asks that four women be remembered: Primilla, Cyriaca, Dorothea and Chreste. The third records that a woman named Akeptous gave the table 'to God Jesus Christ as a memorial'. The words God, Jesus and Christ are abbreviated, as sacred names are in Christian manuscripts.

The excavators called the room a Christian prayer hall and dated it to the 3rd century, before Christianity was legal in the empire. It is a room for worship inside a larger residential building, not a church building of the later kind.

In its own words

The God-loving Akeptous has offered the table to God Jesus Christ as a memorial.Translation as given in the Hebrew University Corpus record for inscription 2 (ed. L. Di Segni). Greek: Προσήνικεν Ἀκεπτοῦς ἡ φιλόθεος τὴν τράπεζαν Θ(ε)ῷ Ἰ(ησο)ῦ Χ(ριστ)ῷ μνημόσυνον.

What the evidence shows

  • Inscription 2 (Akeptous): text and translation as above; SEG 56 (2006) 1900; SEG 61 (2011) 1441; first edition Tepper and Di Segni 2006, pp. 36–41 (Hebrew University corpus).
  • Akeptous was a woman: the Greek gives her the feminine article and adjective, ἡ φιλόθεος. Bowersock calls the feminine name rare and relatively late (2018, p. 68).
  • Inscription 1 (Gaianus): a dedicatory building inscription at the north end of the northern panel, naming Gaianus also called Porphyrius, centurion, and the craftsman; SEG 56 (2006) 1899 (Hebrew University corpus; Bowersock 2018, p. 68).
  • Inscription 3: a request to remember four women, at the east side of the southern panel; SEG 56 (2006) 1901. The corpus notes that the same remembrance formula is used in the synagogue at Dura Europos.
  • The largest panel shows two fish in a roundel (Bowersock 2018, p. 68).
  • A raised platform in the floor, most likely the base of the table named in the Akeptous inscription; Bowersock identifies the table as the place of the Eucharist (2018, pp. 68–69).
  • Coins and pottery put the building of the hall in the early 3rd century, and its end about 300 CE, which the corpus connects with the orderly transfer of the Sixth Legion to Arabia under Diocletian (Hebrew University corpus, site record).

Open questions

  • How early? Tepper and Di Segni date the hall to the early 3rd century; the exhibition gives 'about the year 230'. Bowersock (2018) dates the mosaic to the late 3rd century or very early 4th, and reports that R. Talgam, judging by style, would lean later while granting the excavators' arguments. C. Markschies questioned the 3rd-century date; Bowersock reports that Di Segni answered him in SEG 61, no. 1441. Every date proposed in these sources is earlier than the Council of Nicaea (325).
  • 'God Jesus Christ'. Bowersock observes that calling Christ God in an inscription is rare before Constantine, and uses that as an argument against a date after the 3rd century. The inscription shows what one congregation wrote on its own floor; it does not show how widely the wording was used.
  • A church? The excavators' term is 'prayer hall', and others use aula ecclesiae for such rooms. Bowersock says the space was clearly not a church. The find is a room set aside for Christian worship within another building.
  • Soldiers and Christians. A serving centurion paid for the floor. Bowersock takes this as a sign of Christianity's spread in the army. How the hall stood in relation to the camp's official cults is not known from the finds.

The Find

  • Date3rd century CE. Excavators: hall and mosaic built in the early 3rd century and abandoned about 300 CE. G. W. Bowersock (2018): the end of the 3rd century, or very early in the 4th
  • How datedCoins and pottery from the building (the excavators, as summarised by the Hebrew University corpus); the presence of the Sixth Legion Ferrata at Udhruḥ by about 303–4 CE as a latest date, with the name Akeptous and the spelling of the verb in her inscription (Bowersock 2018, pp. 67–69); Gaianus's double name, a usage common in the 1st–3rd centuries that faded in the 4th (Hebrew University corpus, inscription 1).
  • Discovered2005, Yotam Tepper, Israel Antiquities Authority, with inmates of the prison working on the excavation; inscriptions edited by Leah Di Segni, Megiddo Prison compound, at ancient Kefar 'Othnay beside the legionary base of Legio, near Tel Megiddo, Israel
  • Now heldOn loan from the Israel Antiquities Authority to the Museum of the Bible, Washington DC, in the exhibition 'The Megiddo Mosaic: Foundations of Faith' (opened 15 September 2024; current run listed as 25 July 2025 – 31 December 2026). Press reports say it will then return to Israel for display where it was found
  • MaterialStone mosaic (tesserae) floor
  • DimensionsMosaic about 54 m²; hall about 5 × 10 m
  • ScriptGreek
  • LanguageGreek

  • 1 Corinthians 10:16–21; 11:23–26'The table of the Lord' and the Lord's supper. Akeptous gave a table, and its base stood in the middle of the hall.
  • Acts 10:1–2, 44–48Cornelius, a centurion, is baptised with his household. At Kefar 'Othnay a centurion is named as a brother and as the donor of the floor.
  • Romans 16:1–15Paul greets women of the Roman congregation by name. Five women are named in this hall: the donor of the table and four to be remembered.
