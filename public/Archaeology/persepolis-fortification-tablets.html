Persepolis Fortification Tablets

Administrative archive of the Persepolis region under Darius I · 509-494 BCE

Overview

In 1933-34 Ernst Herzfeld's Oriental Institute team excavated rooms in the fortification wall at the north-east corner of the Persepolis terrace and found tens of thousands of unbaked clay tablets and fragments. They were impregnated with paraffin, shipped to Chicago on loan from Iran, and became available for study in 1937. Richard Hallock published 2,087 Elamite texts in 1969 (OIP 92).

The texts are the day-to-day records of the royal economy of the Persepolis region: issues and transfers of grain, flour, wine, beer, fruit, sheep and cattle as rations to workers, officials, travellers and members of the royal family, across an area reaching at least to Niriz in the south and into Elam in the north. Travellers carried sealed authorizations and the supplier's sealed receipts returned to Persepolis. Letters include orders from Darius himself, e.g. Fort. 6764, where Darius orders 100 sheep to be issued to Irtasduna (Artystone), and PF 1795, 200 marris of wine to her.

Most are in Elamite; a smaller number are in Aramaic, and single tablets are in Greek and Old Persian. The Old Persian tablet (Fort. 1208-101, published by Stolper and Tavernier in 2007) shows Old Persian was written for a routine administrative record, not only for royal monuments. The archive was the subject of Rubin v. Islamic Republic of Iran, in which the US Supreme Court held in 2018 that the collection could not be seized to pay a judgment against Iran.

What the text says (summary)

Administrative Elamite formulae. A typical ration text records that a stated quantity of a commodity (grain, wine, sheep), supplied by a named official, was received by named workers or a traveller at a named place in a given month and year. In a letter (Fort. 6764) Darius orders the issuance of 100 sheep to the princess Irtasduna; in PF 1795 he orders 200 marris (about 2,000 quarts) of wine for her.Summary after R. T. Hallock, Persepolis Fortification Tablets (OIP 92, 1969), Introduction, pp. 1-2 and the letter discussion

What the evidence shows

  • Hallock 1969: Elamite texts deal with transfers of food commodities in 509-494 BCE, years 13-28 of Darius I.
  • Found in the fortification wall at the north-east corner of the Persepolis terrace, excavated 1933/34 by Herzfeld for the Oriental Institute (Hallock 1969, p. 1).
  • Tablets arrived in Chicago in 2,353 numbered boxes plus tins of fragments; 150 complete tablets were returned to Iran in 1948 (Hallock 1969).
  • Hallock published PF 1-2087 (Elamite); the Aramaic texts were assigned to R. A. Bowman.
  • Estimated archive: 15,000-18,000 Elamite documents, 700-1,000 Aramaic, 5,000-6,000 uninscribed sealed tablets (Stolper and Tavernier 2007).
  • Unique items: one Greek tablet (Fort. 1771, 'two marris of wine, Tebet'), one Old Persian tablet (Fort. 1208-101), a Phrygian(?) tablet (A. 29797), and a Neo-Babylonian slave-sale document (Fort. 11786) (Stolper and Tavernier 2007).
  • Royal women act in the texts: Irtasduna (Artystone), wife of Darius, receives and orders commodities (PF 730-32, 1835-39).
  • Rubin v. Islamic Republic of Iran, 583 U.S. 202 (argued 4 Dec 2017, decided 21 Feb 2018): the 'Persepolis Collection' of about 30,000 tablets and fragments at the Oriental Institute is immune from attachment; opinion by Sotomayor, J., Kagan, J., not participating.

Open questions

  • Is there any Judean in the archive? The old entry claimed the tablets 'mention Jewish administrative roles'. None of the sources read here (Hallock 1969 introduction; Stolper and Tavernier 2007) identifies a Judean. The archive's personal names are overwhelmingly Iranian and Elamite; whether any bearer of a West Semitic name is a Judean would need the same name-based test used for the Babylonian archives. On what was checked, the sources are silent.
  • How big was the archive, and how much Aramaic record-keeping is missing? Hallock (1969) raised the 'essentially unanswerable question' of how far records were kept in Aramaic on parchment and are now lost. Stolper and Tavernier (2007) estimate what survives. Both point to a much larger paper trail that did not survive in clay.
  • Women in the Persian economy: Y. Safaee, Women of the Empire: Life and Labor in the Achaemenid Persepolis Archives (Brill, 2026), builds a study of women's rations, work and status on these texts. That is new work on old tablets.

Since 2024

  • 2026-03Y. Safaee published a monograph on women's life and labour in the Persepolis archives (Brill). It is a new study of already-known tablets. No new returns of tablets to Iran or new editions were verified for 2024-2026. [4]

The Find

  • DateYears 13-28 of Darius I, 509-494 BCE (Hallock 1969, for the Elamite texts)
  • How datedRegnal-year and month dates written on the tablets; Hallock notes the identification of the king as Darius I was settled by a text naming Darius and by the calendar data (intercalations).
  • Discovered1933-1934, Oriental Institute (University of Chicago) Persepolis expedition directed by Ernst Herzfeld, Rooms in the fortification wall at the north-east corner of the Persepolis terrace, Fars, Iran
  • Now heldLent by Iran to the Oriental Institute (now ISAC), University of Chicago, in 1937 for study; much of the collection has been returned to Iran in batches (150 complete tablets in 1948, per Hallock). The balance of the loan's location as of 2026 was Not confirmed in a published museum record.
  • MaterialUnbaked clay tablets, fragments and sealed tags/bullae; many sealed with cylinder or stamp seals
  • DimensionsRemains of perhaps 15,000-18,000 Elamite documents, 700-1,000 Aramaic documents, and 5,000-6,000 sealed but uninscribed tablets (Stolper and Tavernier 2007); 2,087 Elamite texts published by Hallock (PF 1-2087); the US Supreme Court described 'approximately 30,000 clay tablets and fragments' at Chicago
  • ScriptElamite cuneiform (the great majority); Aramaic alphabetic script; single items in Greek, Old Persian cuneiform, Phrygian(?) and a Neo-Babylonian legal text
  • LanguageAchaemenid Elamite (majority); Imperial Aramaic; one Old Persian administrative tablet (Fort. 1208-101); one Greek tablet (Fort. 1771)

Biblical Passages

  • Ezra 4:7-23; 5:6-6:12; 7:11-26Ezra reproduces Aramaic letters between Persian officials and the court, orders to release supplies from royal stores (6:8-9: 'wheat, salt, wine, and oil') and to fund the temple from the tribute of Beyond the River. The Fortification tablets are original examples of the same Persian system of sealed orders and commodity issues, in the heartland under Darius I. They do not mention Judah.
  • Neh 2:7-9; 5:14-18Nehemiah asks for letters to governors so they will let him pass and give him timber: the Persepolis travel texts show travellers carrying sealed authorizations that entitle them to rations on the road. Nehemiah's 'bread of the governor' (5:14) is a ration allowance of the same kind. The link is to the system, not to named persons.
  • Esth 1:1-2; 2:3; 3:12-13Esther's world of satrapies, royal couriers and sealed documents under a Persian king is the administrative world these tablets document for Darius I (Xerxes' father). The tablets predate Xerxes and do not name Esther, Mordecai or Haman.
  • Ezra 6:1-12Ezra 6 has Darius (I) confirm Cyrus's decree and order supplies for the Jerusalem temple. The Fortification tablets show Darius I personally issuing orders for commodity disbursements, as in Fort. 6764. The Jerusalem order itself is not in the archive.
