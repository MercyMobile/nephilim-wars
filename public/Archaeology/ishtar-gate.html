Ishtar Gate

Glazed-brick city gate of Nebuchadnezzar II, Babylon · early 6th c. BCE

Overview

The Ishtar Gate was the main northern gate of Nebuchadnezzar II's Babylon, a double gateway through the city's inner walls at the end of the Procession Street. Koldewey excavated it in 1902 and called it 'the largest and most striking ruin of Babylon', its walls still standing 12 metres high.

Its walls carried rows of bulls and dragons (the sirrush, Marduk's animal) in moulded brick relief - first unglazed, then in flat and relief glazed brick as the road level was repeatedly raised. The famous striding lions belong to the walls of the Procession Street leading to the gate, not to the gate itself. A limestone block found between the two doorways bears Nebuchadnezzar's consecration text for the gate.

German excavators shipped glazed-brick fragments to Berlin under the 1903 and 1926 find divisions; conservators reassembled 72 animal reliefs and the front gate, shown in the Pergamonmuseum from 1930. The Berlin building is under renovation: Staatliche Museen zu Berlin say the South Wing closed on 23 October 2023, the museum partly reopens on 4 June 2027, and the Vorderasiatisches Museum's full collection returns with the South Wing, expected in 2037.

What the evidence shows

  • Double gateway: two doorways one behind the other joined into one block, through the paired inner walls (Imgur-Bel and Nimitti-Bel) (Koldewey 1914, pp. 32-34).
  • Decoration: alternating horizontal rows of bulls and sirrush dragons; each animal 13 courses high, repeating every 24 courses (c. 2 m); 9 rows visible from top to bottom (Koldewey 1914, pp. 39-41).
  • The road level was raised at least twice; traces of successive pavements between the animal rows (Koldewey 1914, p. 42).
  • Consecration inscription on a limestone block found between the two doorways at the level of the topmost pavement (Koldewey 1914, p. 45).
  • Lions in glazed relief lined the defensive walls of the Procession Street approaching the gate (Koldewey 1914, p. 26).
  • Nebuchadnezzar's East India House Inscription describes rebuilding the gates of Imgur-Bel and Nimitti-Bel with burnt brick and 'brilliant stones on which bulls and serpents were engraved' and placing bronze bulls and upright serpents at their thresholds (Langdon 1905, p. 131).
  • Archaeomagnetic intensity from five brick specimens across three phases: 136 +/- 2.1 ZAm2, best fit 569 BCE on the Levantine curve; significantly different from the value for the 586 BCE Jerusalem destruction layer (148.9 +/- 3.9 ZAm2) (Di Chiara et al. 2024).
  • Berlin reconstruction built from fragments allotted in the 1903 (Istanbul) and 1926 (Baghdad) find divisions; 72 animal reliefs assembled in two years; displayed from 1930 (SMB).

Open questions

  • Before or after the fall of Jerusalem? Di Chiara, Tauxe, Gries, Helwing, Howland & Ben-Yosef (PLOS ONE 19/1, 2024) find the gate's magnetic intensity differs significantly from that recorded in Jerusalem's 586 BCE destruction layer and fits the curve best at 569 BCE, so they conclude the gate was built after 586 'although probably not immediately after'. The 583 +/- 22 BCE figure sometimes quoted is the age they assigned from Nebuchadnezzar's reign, not an independent measurement. Our note: an intensity-only match on a regional curve can cross the curve at more than one date, so the 569 BCE figure depends on the Levantine curve's shape and resolution for the 6th century.
  • How much of what Berlin shows is the ancient gate? Koldewey (1914) describes the monument as a double gateway with walls still 12 m high; Berlin shows the smaller front gate rebuilt from sorted fragments with modern bricks (SMB). Anyone quoting a height should say whether it is the ruin at Babylon, the Berlin reconstruction, or a reconstruction drawing.

The Find

  • DateReign of Nebuchadnezzar II (604-562 BCE); archaeomagnetic best fit 569 BCE (Di Chiara et al. 2024)
  • How datedBricks of the earliest phase are stamped with Nebuchadnezzar II's name and the gate carried his consecration inscription (Koldewey 1914, p. 45). Di Chiara et al. (PLOS ONE 2024) measured geomagnetic intensity in bricks from all three building phases: the average fits the Levantine archaeomagnetic curve best at 569 BCE; they assign the data point an age of 583 +/- 22 BCE from the reign itself, and conclude the phases were built in quick succession.
  • DiscoveredExcavated February-November 1902 (Deutsche Orient-Gesellschaft excavations at Babylon, 1899-1917), Robert Koldewey, North side of the Kasr mound, Babylon (Iraq), at the end of the Procession Street
  • Now heldFoundations and lower walls in situ at Babylon, Iraq. The front gate and part of the Procession Street were rebuilt from excavated glazed-brick fragments in the Pergamonmuseum / Vorderasiatisches Museum, Berlin (on display from 1930); that wing has been closed since 23 Oct 2023. Other glazed-brick fragments went to Istanbul in the 1903 find division and to Baghdad in 1926 (SMB); single animals from the Procession Street and gate are in several other museums.
  • MaterialBaked brick in bitumen; relief bricks both unglazed-moulded and coloured-glazed
  • DimensionsSurviving walls stood 12 m high at excavation (Koldewey); animal reliefs each 13 brick courses tall, rows 24 courses (c. 2 m) apart; 9 rows of animals visible
  • ScriptNeo-Babylonian cuneiform (consecration inscription; stamped bricks)
  • LanguageAkkadian (Babylonian)

Biblical Passages

  • Dan 4:29-30Nebuchadnezzar walks on his palace and says 'Is not this great Babylon, that I have built'; the gate is one of his signed building works a few hundred metres from the palace.
  • 2 Kgs 24:10-16; 25:8-11Judahite exiles (597 and 586 BCE) were taken to Babylon. If the 2024 archaeomagnetic result holds, the gate standing today was built after the 586 deportation; our reading, not the text's.
  • Jer 51:58'The broad walls of Babylon shall be utterly broken, and her high gates shall be burned with fire' - a prophecy about the walls and gates of this city; the gate is one of those gates.
  • Bel and the Dragon (Greek additions to Daniel)Koldewey (1914, p. 46) himself compared the sirrush, 'the dragon of Babylon' on the gate, with the dragon story; that comparison is his suggestion.
