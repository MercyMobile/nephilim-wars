Ekron Royal Dedicatory Inscription

Limestone temple dedication naming Ekron and five of its rulers · first half of the 7th century BCE

Overview

In 1996 the Tel Miqne-Ekron excavation found a limestone block with a five-line inscription in the destruction debris of Temple Complex 650, a large building (186 by 124 ft) in the elite zone of the lower city. The excavators attribute the destruction to the Babylonian sack of 603 BCE. Seymour Gitin, Trude Dothan and Joseph Naveh published it in Israel Exploration Journal 47 (1997) and summarized it for a general audience in Archaeology (1998).

The text records a temple built by 'kysh (Achish; in Assyrian, Ikausu), son of Padi, son of Ysd, son of Ada, son of Ya'ir, ruler of Ekron, for his lady, a goddess whose name is read Ptgyh. It asks her to bless him, protect him, prolong his days, and bless his land.

Because the inscription names both the city (Ekron) and two rulers known as kings of Ekron from Assyrian records, it settles that Tel Miqne is Ekron. In the same complex a storage jar was found inscribed 'for Baal and for Padi' (Archaeology 1998).

What the text says (summary)

The house that 'kysh son of Padi, son of Ysd, son of Ada, son of Ya'ir, ruler of Ekron, built for Ptgyh his lady. May she bless him and guard him and lengthen his days and bless his land.Literal translation (this encyclopedia) of the published transliteration (Gitin, Dothan and Naveh, IEJ 47, 1997). Compare the editors' English in Archaeology 51/1 (1998). No public-domain translation exists.

What the evidence shows

  • Found in the Babylonian destruction debris of Temple Complex 650, a 186 x 124 ft structure in the elite zone of the lower tel (Gitin, Dothan and Naveh 1998).
  • The text runs five lines, 72 letters by the editors' count in Archaeology (1998).
  • It names the city Ekron ('qrn) and five generations of its rulers: 'kysh, Padi, Ysd, Ada, Ya'ir (same).
  • Padi and Ikausu are named as kings of Ekron in Assyrian royal records. The other three rulers are otherwise unknown (same).
  • A storage jar in a side room of the same temple complex is inscribed 'for Baal and for Padi' (same).
  • The block is porous local limestone, the text is set in a drawn frame, and the engraver added the final he of the goddess's name above a word-divider, apparently as an afterthought (Demsky 1997, from autopsy at the Albright Institute and the Israel Museum).

Open questions

  • The goddess's name. Naveh (IEJ 1997) read Ptgyh, marking the third letter as uncertain, and took it as a non-Semitic (Greek) name. Demsky (JANES 25, 1997), after examining the stone under different lighting, says the supposed small gimel is 'no more than a wedged shaped chip in the porous stone' and reads ptyh. Schaefer-Lichtenberger (1998) proposed Pythogaia (Gaia), and Press (BASOR 365, 2012) argues that identification lacks supporting evidence and rests on the idea of an Aegean 'Great Goddess', which he rejects.
  • The king's name. The editors vocalize 'kysh as Achish, following the Bible, and give Ikausu from Assyrian texts. Naveh derives it from Achaios, 'Achaean/Greek'. Demsky prefers a link with Anchises (Iliad). Both are etymological proposals; the stone gives only the consonants 'kysh.
  • Language. The editors note that a 7th-century Philistine city wrote in a dialect close to Phoenician. Demsky reads it as the local South Canaanite dialect, a forerunner of Nehemiah's 'Ashdodite'. The inscription does not show what language the early Philistines spoke.

Corrected from the earlier edition of this page
  • The inscription is sometimes said to name 'Achish king of Gath' from 1 Samuel. The stone names 'kysh as ruler of Ekron, son of Padi, in the 7th century BCE. The biblical Achish ruled Gath about three centuries earlier.
  • It is often called 'Philistine language'. The dialect is close to Phoenician (the editors) or local South Canaanite (Demsky 1997). It does not preserve the early Philistines' own non-Semitic language.
  • The goddess's name is sometimes given as a settled 'Ptgyh = Pythogaia'. The third letter is contested (Demsky 1997 reads ptyh), and the Gaia identification is disputed (Press 2012).
  • Some online references give IEJ 47: 1-18. Demsky's 1997 footnote gives 1-16.

The Find

  • DateFirst half of the 7th century BCE, during the reign of Ikausu (Achish), son of Padi
  • How datedThe builder 'kysh son of Padi matches two kings of Ekron in Assyrian records. Padi appears in Sennacherib's annals for the 701 BCE campaign, and Assyrian records call both Padi and Ikausu kings of Ekron (Gitin, Dothan and Naveh, Archaeology 51/1, 1998). The Assyrian texts naming Ikausu were not re-read for this entry. The stone was found in the destruction debris the excavators attribute to Nebuchadnezzar's sack of 603 BCE.
  • Discovered1996, Tel Miqne-Ekron excavation directed by Seymour Gitin (W. F. Albright Institute) and Trude Dothan (Hebrew University); text read by Joseph Naveh, Temple Complex 650, elite zone of the lower city, Tel Miqne (Ekron), in the Babylonian destruction debris
  • Now heldThe Israel Museum, Jerusalem (on public display by 1997, per Demsky, JANES 25, 1997); plaster copy at the Tel Ekron Museum, Kibbutz Revadim
  • MaterialPorous local limestone block, probably from the temple wall (Demsky 1997)
  • ScriptAlphabetic script close to Phoenician; five lines within a drawn frame, with word dividers
  • LanguageA Canaanite dialect close to Phoenician, used at Ekron (Demsky calls it South Canaanite and links it to the 'Ashdodite' of Neh 13:24)

Biblical Passages

  • Josh 13:3; 1 Sam 5:10; 6:17Ekron is one of the five Philistine cities. The stone names 'qrn and its ruler, fixing Ekron at Tel Miqne.
  • 1 Sam 21:10-15; 27:2Achish, king of Gath in David's time. The Ekron king 'kysh has the same name in a different city about three centuries later. This is a name parallel, not the same person.
  • 2 Kgs 18:8, 13-16Hezekiah strikes the Philistines, and Sennacherib's 701 BCE campaign follows. Padi, the Ekron builder's father, is the king of Ekron in Sennacherib's account of that campaign (Archaeology 1998); the Bible does not name him.
  • Jer 25:20; Amos 1:8; Zeph 2:4; Zech 9:5-7Prophetic oracles against Ekron. The Babylonian destruction layer in which the stone was found fits the 7th-6th century horizon of Jeremiah and Zephaniah (our reading).
  • Neh 13:24'Half of their children spoke the language of Ashdod'. Demsky (1997) links the inscription's dialect to this Philistine-city speech.
