Sargon II Palace Inscriptions (Khorsabad)

Royal inscriptions from Sargon II's palace at Dūr-Šarrukīn · c. 710-706 BCE

Overview

In March 1843 Paul-Émile Botta, the French consular agent at Mosul, found carved and inscribed stone rooms at Khorsabad. They were the palace of Sargon II in his new capital Dūr-Šarrukīn ('Fortress of Sargon'). Botta dug until 1844, Victor Place continued in 1852-54, and the Oriental Institute of Chicago excavated again in 1928-35. Botta published his copies in Monument de Ninive (1849-50), thinking he had found biblical Nineveh.

The inscriptions include annals carved room by room on wall slabs (Rooms II, V, XIII, XIV and the throne room), the 'Display Inscription' (a summary of the reign to year 15), texts on the bull colossi and pavement slabs, and short captions over reliefs. Grant Frame's edition (RINAP 2, 2021) covers about 130 historical inscriptions of Sargon.

For biblical history the key passages are the capture of Samaria (see separate entry), the revolt of Hamath with Arpad, Ṣimirra, Damascus and Samaria (720 BCE), the defeat of Hanno of Gaza and an Egyptian army at Raphia, and the Ashdod campaign against Iāmānī in 712/711 BCE. The Ashdod campaign is the event Isaiah 20:1 dates by 'the year that Tartan came unto Ashdod, when Sargon the king of Assyria sent him'. Isaiah 20:1 is the only verse in the Bible that names Sargon.

In its own words

(97) In the anger of my heart I did not collect the mass of my troops, (98) I did not rally my forces. (99) With my soldiers, who do not depart from my side, ... (100) I marched against Ashdod. (101) Yamani, who heard from afar (102) the approach of my column, fled to the borders of Egypt, (103) which lies before Melucha, and was seen no more. (104) Ashdod, Gimtu [Gath], Ashdudimmu (105) I besieged [and] conquered.R. W. Rogers, Cuneiform Parallels to the Old Testament (New York, 1912), pp. 328-329 (Display/'General' Inscription ll. 97-105)

What the evidence shows

  • Display Inscription (RINAP 2 no. 7) ll. 90-109: Azūri of Ashdod stops tribute and is replaced by his brother Aḫī-Mīti; the people put up Iāmānī; Sargon marches 'with (only) my warriors who never leave my side'; Iāmānī flees 'to the (far) edge of Egypt, on the border with the land Meluḫḫa'; Ashdod, Gath and Ashdod-Yam are conquered and resettled under an Assyrian governor.
  • Display Inscription ll. 109b-112: the king of Meluḫḫa (Nubia) later sent Iāmānī to Assyria in iron fetters.
  • Khorsabad Annals date the Ashdod campaign to Sargon's 11th regnal year (711 BCE); the Nineveh Prism (RINAP 2 no. 82) puts it in the 9th regnal year (RINAP 2 Introduction, note 96).
  • Display Inscription l. 33: Ilu-bi'dī (Iaū-bi'dī) of Hamath 'incited the cities Arpad, Ṣimirra, Damascus, (and) Samaria to rebel'; Sargon captured him at Qarqar and flayed him.
  • Pavement and bull inscriptions give short titularies: 'conqueror of the city Samaria and all of the land Bīt-Ḫumria (Israel)' (RINAP 2 no. 13); Display Inscription of Room XIV: 'I plundered the city Šinuḫtu, the city Samaria, and all of the land Bīt-Ḫumria' (no. 8).
  • Text preservation: RINAP 2 states that text nos. 1-4, 6-14 and 23-41 are unavailable in the original because the slabs were lost in the Tigris in 1855 or reburied at Khorsabad; for these the edition depends on Botta's copies ('numerous anomalous/unexpected signs') and Winckler's later copies.
  • Botta's room numbering used Roman numerals for spaces he took to be courts; several (II, V, X, XIII, XIV and 'Court VII', the throne room) were actually rooms (RINAP 2 Introduction).

Open questions

  • Who led the Ashdod campaign? Isaiah 20:1 says the turtānu (field marshal) came to Ashdod, sent by Sargon. The Display Inscription has Sargon marching himself 'with (only) my warriors who never leave my side'. RINAP 2 (Frame 2021, n. 118) identifies Isaiah's campaign with the capture of Ashdod in 711 and notes that Isaiah has the army led by the tartan, not the king. Whether the king was actually present, or the royal text credits him with his general's campaign, is not decided by the texts.
  • 711 or 713/712? The Khorsabad Annals put the Ashdod campaign in Sargon's 11th regnal year and the Nineveh Prism in his 9th (RINAP 2 n. 96). Hayim Tadmor (JCS 12, 1958) is the classic chronological study of Sargon's regnal-year (palû) counting; the discrepancy affects how Isaiah 20's 'three years' sign is dated.
  • How reliable is the received text? For most Khorsabad slabs the originals are gone (Tigris 1855) and editors rely on Botta's 1840s copies. Frame's RINAP 2 lists the problems with those copies; any single reading from a lost slab is only as good as Botta's copy.

  • Old entry lists '2 Kings 18-19 (Sargon's campaign)' and '2 Kings 20 (Hezekiah's illness)'. 2 Kings 18:13-19:37 is Sennacherib's 701 BCE campaign and 2 Kings 20 is Hezekiah's illness; neither names Sargon.

  • Old entry dates the inscriptions 'c. 722 BCE'. 722 is Sargon's accession year; the palace texts summarize events down to his 15th year (707) and the city was inaugurated in 706.
  • Old entry's 'Material: Stone inscriptions; Dimensions: Various tablets' conflates media: the palace texts are on stone wall slabs, pavement slabs and bull colossi; the clay prisms (e.g. Nimrud, Nineveh, Aššur) are separate documents from other cities.

The Find

  • DateSargon II reigned 722-705 BCE. The Display Inscription runs 'from the beginning of my reign until my fifteenth regnal year' (707 BCE); the palace at Dūr-Šarrukīn was inaugurated in his 16th year (706 BCE).
  • How datedInternal regnal-year statements: the Display Inscription (RINAP 2 no. 7) says its summary runs to the 15th regnal year. Sargon's accession date comes from the Babylonian Chronicle (12 Ṭebētu, Shalmaneser V's 5th year = 722 BCE), and his regnal years are fixed by the Assyrian eponym list. The RINAP 2 campaign table puts the inauguration of Dūr-Šarrukīn in 706.
  • DiscoveredMarch 1843-1844 (P.-É. Botta); 1852-1854 (V. Place with F. Thomas); 1928-1935 (Oriental Institute of the University of Chicago: E. Chiera, H. Frankfort, G. Loud; epigraphist T. Jacobsen), Paul-Émile Botta, French consular agent at Mosul (not Layard), Khorsabad (ancient Dūr-Šarrukīn), about 15 km north-east of Mosul, Iraq
  • Now heldDispersed: Louvre (Botta's and Place's shipments), British Museum, Oriental Institute Chicago, Iraq Museum and elsewhere. Many inscribed slabs were lost in the Tigris in 1855 or reburied at the site and survive only in Botta's copies (Monument de Ninive, 1849-50).
  • MaterialCarved stone wall slabs (orthostats), stone pavement slabs, human-headed bull colossi; also clay prisms and cylinders from other sites
  • ScriptNeo-Assyrian cuneiform
  • LanguageAkkadian (Standard Babylonian literary dialect of royal inscriptions)

Biblical Passages

  • Isa 20:1-6'In the year that Tartan came unto Ashdod, (when Sargon the king of Assyria sent him,) and fought against Ashdod, and took it.' Sargon's inscriptions record the capture of Ashdod, Gath and Ashdod-Yam after Iāmānī's revolt, with Iāmānī fleeing to the border of Egypt/Meluḫḫa (Kush). Isaiah 20:3-5 addresses Egypt and Ethiopia (Kush) as the failed hope of those who revolted. Our reading: the same revolt and the same Egypt-Kush connection.
  • 2 Kgs 17:6, 24; 18:9-11Deportation from Samaria and resettlement of foreigners there. Sargon's texts claim he took Samaria, deported its people and 'brought there people from the lands that I had conquered'. See the 'Sargon's Annals - Fall of Samaria' entry for who took the city.
  • Isa 10:9'Is not Hamath as Arpad? is not Samaria as Damascus?' Sargon's Display Inscription groups Arpad, Ṣimirra, Damascus and Samaria as the cities Ilu-bi'dī of Hamath incited to revolt.
