Khirbet Qeiyafa Fortifications

Casemate wall and two four-chambered gates · late 11th-early 10th c. BCE

Overview

Khirbet Qeiyafa is a 2.3-hectare hilltop town above the Elah Valley enclosed by a casemate wall of very large stones. The Hebrew University and Israel Antiquities Authority excavated about a fifth of it in 2007-2013 under Yosef Garfinkel and Saar Ganor.

The wall has two gates, west and south, each with four chambers. Millard (2011) notes the west gate was 'inserted where a space had been designed for it as the wall was built', and that the casemate rooms served as back rooms of the houses built against the wall. A Hellenistic occupation lies directly on the Iron Age town with nothing in between.

The Iron Age town was short-lived and ended in a sudden destruction that left hundreds of restorable vessels. Burnt olive pits from that destruction were radiocarbon-dated to about 1000 BCE.

What the evidence shows

  • Casemate wall with megalithic stones; small-stone Hellenistic additions at the top (Millard 2011, p. 4).
  • Two gates, west and south, each with four chambers (project website; Millard 2011).
  • Casemate chambers integrated into houses as back rooms (Millard 2011, p. 4).
  • Single Iron Age phase ending in destruction with 'hundreds of restorable pottery vessels, stone utensils and metal objects' (project website).
  • 17 burnt olive-pit samples from a jar in the destruction layer, some split between two laboratories: '~1000 BC' (Garfinkel et al., Radiocarbon 57, 2015).

Open questions

  • Date: Garfinkel et al. (Radiocarbon 54, 2012; 57, 2015) date the destruction to c. 1000 BCE from on-site olive pits; Finkelstein & Piasetzky (Radiocarbon 57, 2015) combine 14C from six Shephelah sites in a Bayesian model and push Qeiyafa into the 10th c., later than the excavators' estimate.
  • Who built it: Garfinkel & Ganor (JHS 8, 2008) treat it as a Judahite stronghold of David's kingdom; Na'aman (JHS 17, 2017) argues nothing in the published finds ties it to Jerusalem or the highlands and that its trade links point to a Shephelah context.
  • Identification: Garfinkel & Ganor (JHS 8, 2008) identify it as Sha'arayim ('two gates') because it is the only Iron Age site in Judah or Israel with two gates. Other identifications have been proposed (Millard 2011, p. 4, notes this without detail). No name is written at the site.

The Find

  • DateSingle Iron Age phase; olive pits from the destruction layer give c. 1000 BCE (Garfinkel et al. 2015). Finkelstein & Piasetzky's regional model places the site later, in the 10th c. BCE.
  • How datedAMS 14C on short-lived samples (burnt olive pits) from the destruction layer (Radiocarbon 54, 2012; 57, 2015), plus Iron I/IIA pottery; disputed by a regional Bayesian model (Finkelstein & Piasetzky, Radiocarbon 57, 2015).
  • DiscoveredExcavated 2007-2013 (five seasons), Yosef Garfinkel (Hebrew University of Jerusalem) and Saar Ganor (Israel Antiquities Authority), Hilltop on the north side of the Elah Valley, between Azekah and Socoh, Judean Shephelah, Israel
  • Now heldIn situ, Khirbet Qeiyafa, Elah Valley, Israel
  • MaterialCasemate wall of large (megalithic) stones; Hellenistic additions in smaller stones on top
  • DimensionsEnclosed area 2.3 ha; c. 200 m of the casemate wall exposed

Biblical Passages

  • 1 Sam 17:1-2, 52Places the Philistine-Israelite confrontation in the Elah Valley between Socoh and Azekah and names Sha'arayim on the Philistine retreat route. The site sits in that valley and has two gates; the name identification is the excavators' reading.
  • Josh 15:36Lists Sha'arayim among Shephelah towns of Judah near Azekah and Socoh.
