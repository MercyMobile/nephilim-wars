Black Obelisk of Shalmaneser III

Assyrian four-sided tribute monument from Nimrud · erected c. 825 BCE

Overview

A four-sided black limestone monument, just under two metres tall, set up in a courtyard at Kalhu (Nimrud), Assyria's capital, near the end of Shalmaneser III's reign. It carries a long annalistic inscription covering his campaigns year by year down to his 31st regnal year, and five horizontal registers of relief that wrap around all four faces. Each register shows a named vassal delivering payment, with a short cuneiform caption (epigraph) above it.

The five payers are Sua of Gilzanu, Iaua (Jehu) 'son of Omri', the land of Muṣri (exotic animals: camels, buffalo, rhinoceros, elephants, monkeys), Marduk-apla-uṣur of Suhu, and Qarparunda of Patina. In the second register a man crouches 'in a pose of tribute' (Oracc Nimrud project) at the king's feet; the caption names the payer as Iaua of Bīt-Ḫumrî, whom the texts pair with Tyre and Sidon in the year of the campaign against Hazael of Damascus.

Layard's crew found the obelisk lying on its side about ten feet down in 1846, and it was shipped to London the same year. Its later campaigns are credited heavily to the field marshal Dayyān-Aššur rather than the king, which the Oracc Nimrud project links to the civil war at the end of Shalmaneser's reign.

In its own words

Tribute of Iaua (Jehu), son of Omri (mâr Humri). Silver, gold, a golden bowl, a golden beaker, golden goblets, pitchers of gold, lead, staves for the hand of the king, javelins, I received from him.D. D. Luckenbill, Ancient Records of Assyria and Babylonia I (Chicago, 1926), §590, p. 211 (epigraph II)

What the evidence shows

  • Epigraph over register 2, transliterated: ma-da-tu šá mia-ú-a DUMU mḫu-um-ri-i ... am-ḫur-šu, literally 'payment of Iāūa son (DUMU) of Ḫumrî ... I received from him' (RIMA 3 A.0.102.88).
  • Items listed for Iaua: silver, gold, a gold bowl (saplu), a gold tureen (zuqūtu), gold vessels (qabuātu), gold pails (dalāni), tin, staffs of the king's hand, spears.
  • Register 2 relief: one figure crouched at the feet of the king, who stands under a parasol; tribute-bearers carrying vessels continue around the other three faces of the same register.
  • The long annal text on the obelisk (A.0.102.14) records the 18th-year battle with Hazael of Damascus (1,121 chariots and 470 cavalry captured) but does not itself name Jehu in that passage; the Jehu name appears only in the epigraph.
  • Parallel annals name the same payer: 'the payment of the people of Tyre (and) Sidon, (as well as of) Jehu (Iāūa) of (Bīt-)Ḫumrî' after the king set up a statue at Mount Ba'ali-ra'asi (A.0.102.8); A.0.102.10 pairs him with Ba'al-manzer of Tyre.
  • Layard's account: 'The corner was part of an obelisk, about six feet six inches in height, lying on its side, ten feet below the surface' (Nineveh and its Remains, quoted by the Oracc Nimrud project).
  • Findspot: Layard's Trench J, centre of the Nimrud citadel; Rassam found a second (broken) obelisk in the same area the following year.
  • Schneider's notes list the other published texts naming 'Jehu son of Omri' in the 841 BCE campaign: the Aleppo fragment (Michel, WO 1/4, 1949), the Kurba'il statue (Kinnier Wilson, Iraq 24, 1962) and the Safar annals (Safar, Sumer 7, 1951).

Open questions

  • Who is 'Iaua son of Omri'? P. Kyle McCarter (BASOR 216, 1974) argued the name could render Joram (Yaw-) rather than Jehu, since Jehu was not of Omri's line; Edwin R. Thiele (BASOR 222, 1976) answered on chronological grounds and Manfred Weippert (Vetus Testamentum 28, 1978) re-examined the spelling and kept Jehu. The Assyrian parallels put the payment in the 18th year (841 BCE), which on the biblical reckoning is Jehu's accession year.
  • Why 'son of Omri'? One reading takes DUMU Ḫumrî as the Assyrian name for the state (Bīt-Ḫumrî, 'House of Omri'), not a genealogy; that is how the RIAo translation renders it. Tammi Schneider (BAR 21:1, Jan/Feb 1995, 'Did King Jehu Kill His Own Family?') instead took the phrase at face value as possible descent from Omri, noting that four Assyrian texts (the obelisk caption, the Aleppo fragment, the Kurba'il statue and the Safar annals) all call him 'son of Omri'. The inscription itself says only DUMU Ḫumrî.
  • Is the prostrate figure Jehu himself or an envoy? The caption names Iaua as the payer but does not say he was present in person; the Oracc Nimrud project describes the scene as Jehu's own gesture, and the old entry's 'Israelite servants' are not identified as such in the text.

  • 'Only contemporary image of a biblical Hebrew king' is stated as fact; the only thing the stone shows is a payer captioned as Iaua, who may be the king or his envoy.

The Find

  • DateMain text runs through Shalmaneser III's 31st regnal year (828 BCE); monument dated c. 825 BCE; the Jehu tribute belongs to his 18th regnal year (841 BCE)
  • How datedRegnal-year formula in the text itself: the annals on the obelisk end with 'In my thirty-first regnal year' (RIMA 3 A.0.102.14 l. 174b). The 18th-year date for Jehu's payment comes from parallel annals (RIMA 3 A.0.102.8 and A.0.102.10), which place 'the payment of ... Jehu (Iāūa) of (Bīt-)Ḫumrî' in the 18th-year campaign against Hazael; Assyrian regnal years are tied to absolute dates through the eponym list.
  • Discovered1846, Austen Henry Layard's excavation team (found by the workmen's superintendent as Layard was leaving the trench), Nimrud (ancient Kalhu), centre of the citadel mound, Layard's Trench J, in a courtyard beside the so-called Central Building
  • Now heldBritish Museum, London, BM 118885 (registration 1848,1104.1)
  • MaterialBlack limestone
  • DimensionsJust under 2 m high (Layard: 'about six feet six inches'); four faces, each c. 45 cm wide; stepped (ziggurat-like) top
  • ScriptNeo-Assyrian cuneiform
  • LanguageAkkadian (Neo-Assyrian royal-inscription dialect)

Biblical Passages

  • 2 Kgs 9-10The epigraph names a ruler Iaua of the House of Omri paying tribute; 2 Kings names Jehu as the king who ended Omri's dynasty. Our reading: same man. The Bible does not mention this tribute.
  • 2 Kgs 10:32-33Hazael pressing Israel in Jehu's reign; the obelisk annals record Shalmaneser fighting Hazael of Damascus in the same 18th-year campaign.
  • 2 Kgs 8:7-15Hazael as king of Damascus: the obelisk text names Hazael (ḫa-za-aʾ-DINGIR) as Shalmaneser's opponent.
