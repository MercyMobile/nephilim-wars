Siloam Tunnel Inscription

Hebrew rock inscription from the Gihon-Siloam tunnel · late 8th c. BCE (debated)

Overview

A six-line Hebrew inscription carved into the wall of the rock-cut water channel that carries the Gihon Spring's water under the City of David to the Siloam Pool. It was noticed in 1880 a few metres in from the tunnel's southern end and published by A. H. Sayce in the Palestine Exploration Fund Quarterly Statement in 1881.

The text is a builders' record, not a royal dedication: it describes the last moments of cutting, when two crews tunnelling toward each other could hear each other's voices through the rock, then broke through 'axe against axe', after which the water flowed 1,200 cubits from the spring to the pool, under 100 cubits of rock. No king, date or god is named.

The inscription was hacked out of the wall in 1890, broke into pieces, was recovered by the Ottoman authorities and taken to Constantinople, where it remains in the Istanbul Archaeology Museums. In September 2025 Turkey's president publicly refused Israel's renewed request for its return.

In its own words

The boring through [is completed]. And this is the story of the boring through: while yet [they plied] the drill, each toward his fellow, and while yet there were three cubits to be bored through, there was heard the voice of one calling unto another ... and the waters flowed from the source to the pool for a thousand and two hundred cubits.G. A. Barton, Archaeology and the Bible (Philadelphia: American Sunday-School Union, 1916; 2nd ed. 1917), Part II, ch. 12, p. 377

What the evidence shows

  • Six lines of Old Hebrew carved on a smoothed patch of the tunnel wall near the Siloam end.
  • Content: two crews cutting toward each other; 3 cubits left when voices were heard; a 'crevice'/fissure in the rock on the right; breakthrough 'axe against axe'; water flowed 1,200 cubits from spring (motsa) to pool (berekah); 100 cubits of rock overhead (Barton 1917, p. 377).
  • No king, regnal year, deity or patron is named in the text.
  • Barton (1917) records the tunnel as 'about 1,700 feet long' after the Parker expedition cleared it (1909-11); the inscription's own figure is 1,200 cubits.
  • Radiocarbon dates on plant remains in the channel plaster and U-Th dates on speleothems put the tunnel's construction around 700 BCE (Frumkin, Shimron & Rosenbaum 2003).
  • The stone was cut out of the wall in 1890, breaking it; it has been in Constantinople/Istanbul since (Barton 1917: 'now in the Imperial Ottoman Museum at Constantinople').

Open questions

  • Who dug it and when. Frumkin, Shimron & Rosenbaum (Nature 425, 2003) date the tunnel to c. 700 BCE from 14C on organic material in the original plaster and U-Th on stalactites, which fits Hezekiah. Reich & Shukron (Tel Aviv 38, 2011) argue completion was at least several decades earlier, in the early 8th century, from pottery in the fills they excavated beside the Gihon Spring, which would mean the tunnel predates Hezekiah. Finkelstein republished a response on 'The Finds from the Rock-Cut Pool in Jerusalem and the Date of the Siloam Tunnel' (2024 collected essays); the chapter's existence is confirmed, but its argument is not summarized here.
  • Paleography vs. a Hasmonean date. Rogerson & Davies (Biblical Archaeologist 59, 1996) proposed the tunnel and inscription could be 2nd-century BCE; Hendel (Biblical Archaeologist 59, 1996) and Cahill (1997) replied that the letter-forms are Iron Age Hebrew. The later radiometric dates (2003) are consistent with the Iron Age side.
  • Is 2 Kings 20:20 describing THIS tunnel? The verse credits Hezekiah with 'a pool, and a conduit' bringing water into the city; 2 Chr 32:30 says he 'stopped the upper watercourse of Gihon, and brought it straight down to the west side of the city'. The tunnel runs from the Gihon to a pool on the west side of the City of David, which matches the description; the inscription itself does not name Hezekiah, so the identification rests on the correspondence, not on the text.
  • The 2025 Siloam Dam dates (805-795 BCE, Regev et al., PNAS 122/35) show that major Gihon-Siloam waterworks already existed c. 800 BCE; they date the dam, not the tunnel, so they do not settle the tunnel's date either way.

The Find

  • DateUndated text; placed c. 700 BCE by the radiometric dates for the tunnel and by letter-forms; other workers argue for earlier (early 8th c.) or much later dates
  • How datedThe inscription names no king and no year. Its date is inferred (1) from the tunnel it was cut into: radiocarbon on plant material in the tunnel plaster and U-Th on speleothems (Frumkin, Shimron & Rosenbaum, Nature 2003) point to c. 700 BCE; (2) from Old Hebrew letter-forms; (3) from pottery in fills near the Gihon Spring, which Reich & Shukron (2011) read as pushing completion back to the early 8th century.
  • Discovered1880, A local youth exploring the tunnel (commonly identified as a pupil of Conrad Schick); first published by A. H. Sayce, 1881, Right (east) wall of the tunnel near its southern (Siloam Pool) end, City of David, Jerusalem
  • Now heldIstanbul Archaeology Museums (cut out of the tunnel wall in 1890 and taken to the Imperial Ottoman Museum, Constantinople). Inventory number reported in secondary listings as 2195 T; not checked against a museum record.
  • MaterialLimestone bedrock of the tunnel wall (now a cut-out, broken slab)
  • ScriptOld Hebrew (Paleo-Hebrew), words divided by dots
  • LanguageHebrew

Biblical Passages

  • 2 Kgs 20:20Credits Hezekiah with making 'a pool, and a conduit' and bringing water into the city; the tunnel is a conduit delivering Gihon water to a pool. The inscription itself names no king.
  • 2 Chr 32:2-4, 30Hezekiah stops the springs outside the city before the Assyrian siege and brings the Gihon water 'straight down to the west side of the city of David' - the course of this tunnel.
  • Isa 22:9-11Speaks of gathering 'the waters of the lower pool' and making 'a ditch between the two walls for the water of the old pool' in a siege context; our reading: a general fit with Jerusalem waterworks, not a specific reference to the inscription.
