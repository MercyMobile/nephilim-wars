Wadi Daliyeh Papyri

Aramaic legal deeds from Persian-period Samaria, hidden in a cave c. 332/331 BCE

Overview

In 1962 Taʿâmireh Bedouin brought papyri, bullae and coins from a cave in Wadi ed-Daliyeh, north of Jericho, to the antiquities dealer Kando. The lot was bought for the Palestine Archaeological Museum (now the Rockefeller Museum) in November 1962 and August 1963. Paul and Nancy Lapp then excavated the cave, Mughâret Abū Shinjeh, for ASOR in 1963-1964 and found more fragments, bullae, and the remains of a large number of people.

The documents are Aramaic legal deeds written 'in Samaria the fortress/city, in Samaria the province' in the last decades of Persian rule (c. 375-335 BCE). Most are sales of slaves, single men, women or groups. Others record a loan (WDSP 17), a lawsuit (WDSP 11 verso), a house sale and other contracts. Dušek distinguishes three successive family archives among them. Witness lists and sealings name governors of Samaria: Ḥananyah (WDSP 7, dated 4 March 354 BCE) and a bulla (WD 22) of '[Dela]yahu son of [Sanba]llat, governor of Samaria'.

The excavators and editors explain the deposit by the events of 332/331 BCE. Quintus Curtius (4.8.9-11) reports that the Samarians burned Alexander's governor Andromachus alive and that Alexander returned to punish them. Refugees from Samaria carrying their deeds would have fled to the cave. Cross (1974) proposed that they were found there and suffocated by a fire lit at the entrance. The cave's contents fit that sequence, but no text in the cave records it.

What the evidence shows

  • Findspot: Mughâret Abū Shinjeh, Wadi ed-Daliyeh, about 14 km north of Jericho. The papyri were bought for the Palestine Archaeological Museum on 19 Nov 1962 and 7 Aug 1963 (Dušek 2020).
  • Place of writing: 'Samaria the fortress/city' in 'Samaria the province' (šmryn byrtʾ/qrytʾ, šmryn mdyntʾ) (Dušek 2020).
  • Dated deeds run from c. 375-365 BCE (WDSP 22) to 19 March 335 BCE (WDSP 1). The kings named are Artaxerxes II, Artaxerxes III and Arses/Darius III (Puech 2003; Dušek 2020).
  • Content: at least half are slave sales. There are also a house sale (pap. 15), rooms in a public building (pap. 14), a vineyard (pap. 16), a loan receipt (pap. 17) and a lawsuit (WDSP 11v) (Puech 2003; Dušek 2020).
  • Governors of Samaria named: Ḥananyah or ʿAnanyah, as witness in WDSP 7 (4 March 354 BCE), and the bulla WD 22 of '[Dela]yahu son of [Sanba]llat, governor of Samaria', attached to WDSP 16 (Dušek 2020).
  • The latest coin is from Tyre, 334 BCE (year 15 of Ozimilk), which gives a terminus post quem for the deposit (Puech 2003).
  • Human remains: Lapp's early report, from Bedouin interviews, gave about 205 skeletons, and after excavation about 300 men, women and children were reported (as summarized by Mendonça 2024, citing Lapp 1965 and Lapp & Lapp 1974). Signs of fire were found at the cave entrance.

Open questions

  • How many Sanballats? F. M. Cross (JBL 94, 1975) used the Daliyeh material to reconstruct a line of governors that repeats the name Sanballat across generations, to fit Josephus' Sanballat in Alexander's time. This summary of Cross comes from secondary accounts; the article was not read. J. Dušek (2007; Religions 11, 2020) reads bulla WD 22 as probably belonging to Delayah son of Sinʾuballiṭ, the same Delayah named with his father in the Elephantine letter of 407 BCE. He argues that Josephus confused events, dating the Gerizim sanctuary a century too late, so no second or third Sanballat is needed. The evidence is one broken bulla legend with the patronym restored, the Elephantine letter, and Josephus Ant. 11.
  • Who died in the cave, and how? Cross (1974) proposed Samarian refugees trapped and suffocated by Alexander's troops, using Curtius 4.8.9-11 and the fire at the entrance. The documents are deeds carried by their owners and do not describe the event. The link to Alexander rests on the terminal dates (335 BCE deed, 334 BCE coin) and Curtius's report.
  • What faith did the Samarian owners hold? Dušek (2020) analyses the personal names in the deeds and finds them largely Yahwistic or West Semitic. That is evidence for Samarian religion before the Samaritan–Jewish split. The deeds themselves contain no cultic texts.

Since 2024

  • 2024-03Mendonça publishes a study of the Daliyeh slave-sale deeds as evidence for the slave trade in Persian Samaria. It is a new analysis of the published texts, with no new fragments. [7]

The Find

  • DateWritten c. 375-335 BCE. The oldest explicitly datable deed is WDSP 22 (c. 375-365 BCE). The latest dated is WDSP 1, 19 March 335 BCE (20 Adar, year 2 of Arses/Artaxerxes IV, at the start of Darius III's reign). WDSP 36 frg. 1 may be later, 335-332 BCE.
  • How datedRegnal-year dating formulas in the deeds themselves (Artaxerxes II, Artaxerxes III, Arses/Darius III). The latest pre-Alexander coin in the cave was struck at Tyre in 334 BCE, in year 15 of Ozimilk (Puech 2003, reviewing Gropp 2001; Dušek 2020).
  • DiscoveredFound by Taʿâmireh Bedouin in or before 1962. Purchased 19 Nov 1962 and 7 Aug 1963. Cave excavated 1963-1964., Taʿâmireh Bedouin (finders); dealer Kando; Yusef Saad, Roland de Vaux and Paul W. Lapp (acquisition); P. W. and N. L. Lapp (ASOR excavations); F. M. Cross (first study), Mughâret Abū Shinjeh, a cave in Wadi ed-Daliyeh, about 14 km north of Jericho
  • Now heldRockefeller Museum (formerly Palestine Archaeological Museum), Jerusalem. A few further fragments are in the Schøyen Collection (Dušek 2020).
  • MaterialPapyrus, with clay bullae (sealings), some still attached
  • DimensionsNone complete. The best-preserved deed, WDSP 1, is about half of its original length. WDSP 28-37 are groups of fragments (Dušek 2020). Gropp's edition (DJD 28) presents 37 entries, 18 of them substantial enough to call papyri (Puech 2003).
  • ScriptAramaic cursive (the bulla WD 22 is in palaeo-Hebrew script)
  • LanguageAramaic (legal formulary with Neo-Babylonian loans); the bulla is Hebrew

Biblical Passages

  • Neh 2:10, 19; 4:1-2; 6:1-14; 13:28Sanballat the Horonite, Nehemiah's opponent, whose daughter married into the high-priestly family. Bulla WD 22 names '[Dela]yahu son of [Sanba]llat, governor of Samaria'. On Dušek's reading this is Sanballat's son, known also from Elephantine (407 BCE). The Bible never calls Sanballat 'governor', but in Neh 4:2 he speaks 'before… the army of Samaria'.
  • Ezra 4:8-10, 17Persian-period letters about Samaria written in Aramaic. The Daliyeh deeds show Aramaic as the working legal language of the province of Samaria in the 4th century BCE.
  • 2 Kgs 17:24-41The account of Samaria's mixed population and worship. The papyri's many Yahwistic personal names in Samaria are data for the religion of the province. How they bear on this passage is an interpretation.
