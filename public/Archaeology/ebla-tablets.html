Ebla Tablets (Royal Archives of Palace G)

Third-millennium cuneiform archive from the royal palace at Tell Mardikh, Syria

Overview

In 1974-1975 the Italian mission led by Paolo Matthiae found thousands of cuneiform tablets and fragments in the royal Palace G at Tell Mardikh, identifying the site as the city of Ebla. The epigraphist Giovanni Pettinato reported on them in The Biblical Archaeologist in 1976, and Alfonso Archi later took over much of the publication (see Archi 2015). Matthiae published a new account of how the tablets were found, distributed and shelved in Studia Eblaitica 11 (2025).

The texts are overwhelmingly administrative: records of textiles, metals and livestock, with letters, treaties, ritual texts and school lexical lists, some bilingual Sumerian-Eblaite. They document Ebla's dealings with dozens of Syrian and Mesopotamian cities in the Early Bronze Age.

In the late 1970s Pettinato and D. N. Freedman announced links to Genesis - names like the patriarchs', and the five 'Cities of the Plain'. Archi and others disputed these readings in print (Biblical Archaeologist 1980-1981). The disputes are about individual readings of cuneiform signs, and are recorded here as disputes, not as findings.

What the evidence shows

  • Find-spot: Palace G on the acropolis of Tell Mardikh; the archives' finding and arrangement are the subject of Matthiae, Studia Eblaitica 11 (2025) 1-20.
  • Discovery in the mid-1970s: Sapienza University (Sept. 2024) dates the discovery of 'the cuneiform tablets from the royal archives' to almost 50 years earlier.
  • Language and script: cuneiform used for Sumerian and for the local Semitic language, Eblaite (the subject of Archi 2015; details not re-reviewed for this entry).
  • Since 2011 the site was damaged: Sapienza (2024) reports that it was 'turned into a military base' with 'clandestine excavation and destruction' in 2014-2019, followed by a 2024 recovery mission.

Open questions

  • Cities of the Plain: D. N. Freedman ('The Real Story of the Ebla Tablets: Ebla and the Cities of the Plain,' BA 41, 1978, 143-164) presented Pettinato's readings of Sodom, Gomorrah, Admah, Zeboiim and Bela in an Ebla tablet. A. Archi ('Further Concerning Ebla and the Bible,' BA 44, 1981, 145-154) and Pettinato himself ('Ebla and the Bible,' BA 43, 1980, 203-216) returned to the question; Freedman summarised the state of the epigraphic evidence in BA 43 (1980) 200-203. The texts of these articles were not available for this entry, so which readings each finally held cannot be stated here from the sources. The tablet sign-readings are the evidence; the published editions (ARET) are where they must be checked.
  • Personal names: early announcements compared Ebla names with Abraham, Ishmael, Eber, Saul, David and names ending in -ya (read as Yah). Whether -ya is a divine name or a hypocoristic ending is a question of Eblaite grammar; the same BA exchange (1978-1981) is the place the argument was made. Not resolved from sources read here.
  • 'Canaan': the old entry says the tablets contain an early reference to Canaan. No primary source cited here confirms a reading of Canaan in the Ebla texts; left open.

The Find

  • DateEarly Bronze Age, the generations before the destruction of Palace G (third millennium BCE; commonly placed in the 24th century BCE)
  • How datedStratigraphy of Palace G at Tell Mardikh (the tablets lay in the palace's destruction level) and synchronisms in the texts with Mesopotamian rulers. Exact BCE figures depend on the Mesopotamian chronology used and on which king destroyed the palace, which the Ebla texts themselves do not record.
  • Discovered1974-1975 (first tablets 1974; main archive 1975), Italian Archaeological Expedition to Syria, Sapienza University of Rome, directed by Paolo Matthiae; epigraphist Giovanni Pettinato (later Alfonso Archi), Palace G, acropolis of Tell Mardikh (ancient Ebla), northern Syria
  • Now heldSyria (Syrian national collections). The specific museums holding the tablets are not confirmed from a primary source.
  • MaterialClay tablets
  • ScriptCuneiform (Sumerian logograms and syllabic signs)
  • LanguageEblaite (a Semitic language) and Sumerian

Biblical Passages

  • Genesis 10-11; 14Ebla shows a literate Semitic-speaking kingdom in Syria with wide trade and treaty relations, centuries before the patriarchal setting. Specific links to Genesis (names, the Cities of the Plain) depend on disputed sign readings and are not established by any source read here.
