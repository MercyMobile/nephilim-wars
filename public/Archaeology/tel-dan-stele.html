Tel Dan Stele

Old Aramaic victory stele fragments naming the "House of David" · 9th century BCE

Overview

Three inscribed basalt fragments found in the Iron Age gate area (Area A) at Tel Dan. The large fragment A was spotted on 21 July 1993 lying in the flagstone pavement in front of the outer gate; its tip is visible in photographs taken a year earlier. Two smaller pieces, B1 and B2, which physically join each other, were found in June 1994 in debris and at the foot of a wall nearby. Avraham Biran and Joseph Naveh published A in Israel Exploration Journal 43 (1993) and B1+B2 in IEJ 45 (1995), and glued the three together in the arrangement still shown in the museum.

The text is a first-person royal victory inscription in Old Aramaic. The speaker says his father 'lay down' (died), that the king of Israel had earlier entered his father's land, that the god Hadad made him king and went before him, and that he killed kings and chariotry. Fragment A preserves the phrases mlk yśrʾl, 'king of Israel' (line 8), and bytdwd, 'House of David' (line 9). Fragment B preserves the ends of two royal names followed by 'son of' ('...rm br' and '...yhw br'), which the editors restored as [Jeho]ram son of [Ahab] and [Ahaz]iahu son of [Jehoram]. The speaker's own name does not survive; the attribution to Hazael of Damascus is an inference from the content.

In 2024 Michael Langlois (University of Strasbourg), using new RTI imaging, 3D scanning and a letter-by-letter chart, argued that fragment A and fragments B1+B2 were cut with two different ductus (letter-stroke habits) and different line spacing, that A and B1 have no real physical join, and that the editors' side-by-side placement 'must be abandoned.' The words 'king of Israel' and 'House of David' on fragment A are not affected by this; the restored pairing of those titles with the name fragments on B is.

What the text says (summary)

Content of the preserved lines (editors' 1995 arrangement): the speaker's father died; the king of Israel had previously entered his father's land; Hadad made the speaker king and went before him; he killed [seve]nty kings with thousands of chariots and horsemen; '...]rm son of [...' king of Israel; '...]yhw son of [...' ... of bytdwd ('House of David'); he laid waste their land; a broken reference to someone ruling over Israel; and a siege.Paraphrase of the Old Aramaic text as published by Biran and Naveh, IEJ 43 (1993) 81-98 and IEJ 45 (1995) 1-18; no public-domain translation exists (the text was found in 1993). Aramaic words mlk yśrʾl (l. 8) and bytdwd (l. 9) as cited by Langlois, IEJ 74/2 (2024) 59.

What the evidence shows

  • Fragment A was found 21 July 1993 in the eastern part of the flagstone pavement before the outer gate in Area A; the pavement had been exposed from 1992 and the fragment's tip shows in earlier photographs (Langlois 2024: 60, citing Biran and Naveh 1993: 81).
  • Fragment B1 was found 20 June 1994 in debris 0.80 m above the pavement, c. 13 m northeast of A; B2 on 30 June 1994, c. 8 m north, at the foot of a wall (Langlois 2024: 60, citing Biran and Naveh 1995: 2, 5).
  • The stone is basalt; the joined fragments measure H 34 x W 32 cm (Israel Museum record).
  • Line 8 of fragment A reads mlk yśrʾl, 'king of Israel'; line 9 reads bytdwd, 'House of David', written as one word string.
  • Fragment B preserves only the ends of two names followed by br, 'son of'; the full names Joram/Jehoram and Ahaziah are editorial restorations.
  • B1 and B2 join physically. A and B1 do not: the editors wrote that 'Fragments A and B cannot be joined in an obvious, unequivocal way' (Biran and Naveh 1995: 11, quoted by Langlois 2024: 62), and a restorer who glued them told the 2024 conference they 'do not really join' (Langlois 2024: 63).
  • Every beth on A has a rounded one-stroke descender; every beth on B1+B2 has two straight strokes meeting at an elbow; lamed, kaph and resh differ in the same A-versus-B split (Langlois 2024: 65-70, RTI close-ups).
  • Lines on A slope downward and are corrected over the first 7-8 lines; lines on B1+B2 are level and evenly spaced, with spacing that does not match A (Langlois 2024: 70-71).
  • Paleomagnetic measurement (Yoav Vaknin, reported by Langlois 2024: 72, 75 as personal communication) found A and B1 have the same magnetic orientation, i.e. cut from the same block in the same orientation.
  • The king who set up the stele is not named in any preserved line.

Open questions

  • Placement of the fragments. Biran and Naveh (IEJ 45, 1995) set B1+B2 beside A so that the name endings on B run into 'king of Israel' and 'House of David' on A, a placement they reached by testing and by a join on the back 'corroborated by three experienced restorers.' Langlois (IEJ 74/2, 2024: 59-79) reads A and B as different hands or a changed hand, because every beth, lamed, kaph and resh shows a systematic A-vs-B difference in ductus and the line slope/spacing differs, and concludes B 'does not continue the lines started by frag. A'. Galil (Palestine Exploration Quarterly 133, 2001: 16-21) had earlier put A below B on literary grounds (as reported by Langlois 2024: 75 n. 18). Our reading: if Langlois is right, the stone still says 'king of Israel' and 'House of David', but the restored pairing 'Joram ... king of Israel' / 'Ahaziah ... House of David' is no longer supported by the stone.
  • Who killed Joram and Ahaziah. The editors' restoration has the Aramaean king claim both deaths ('I killed'). 2 Kings 9:24, 27 has Jehu kill both. Schniedewind (BASOR 302, 1996: 75-90) reads the two accounts together as Jehu acting in collusion with Hazael. The Jewish Museum's 2024 label states the text 'is not consistent with the biblical account of 2 Kings 9.' This dispute rests entirely on the restored names, and so on the placement question above.
  • Authenticity of the find circumstances. Elgvin (Warrior, King, Servant, Savior, 2022: 14 n. 24, as cited by Langlois 2024: 75) raised the possibility that the fragments are modern forgeries, given the timing and separate findspots. Langlois (2024: 75) answers that the difference in ductus 'is not in itself evidence of forgery' and that the discovery circumstances need further research; the fragment A tip visible in photographs a year before its discovery bears on this.
  • Author of the stele. Hazael of Damascus is the usual attribution (Schniedewind 1996; the Jewish Museum label says 'Hazael's name is not mentioned in the inscription'). Other Damascene kings (Ben-Hadad/Bar-Hadad) have been proposed; the preserved text names only the god Hadad, the speaker's unnamed father, and the kingdoms.
  • Whether bytdwd means 'House of David' (a dynasty named after a founder, like 'House of Omri' for Israel in Assyrian texts) or a place-name ('Beth-David'), and whether the one-word spelling matters: Athas (The Tel Dan Inscription, JSOT Sup 360, 2003) reappraised the reading; the arguments were not re-verified for this entry.

The Find

  • Date9th century BCE (Iron Age II); the kings named in the restored text point to c. 841 BCE or shortly after
  • How datedNo date survives on the stone and the king who set it up is not named in the preserved text. The date rests on (1) letter forms (Old Aramaic in the Phoenician-derived alphabet, compared with dated 9th-century inscriptions); (2) the find contexts in Area A at Tel Dan, all below/against Iron Age gate architecture; and (3) the restored royal names on fragment B ("...rm son of" / "...yhw son of"), which the editors restored as Joram of Israel and Ahaziah of Judah, both of whom 2 Kings 9 places in the same year. Point (3) depends on the editors' placement of fragment B beside fragment A, which Langlois (IEJ 2024) argues must be abandoned.
  • DiscoveredFragment A: 21 July 1993. Fragment B1: 20 June 1994. Fragment B2: 30 June 1994., Tel Dan excavation of the Nelson Glueck School of Biblical Archaeology, Hebrew Union College-Jewish Institute of Religion, directed by Avraham Biran; fragments A and B2 spotted by the excavation surveyor Gila Cook, Area A, Tel Dan (northern Israel): A in the eastern part of the flagstone pavement in front of the outer gate; B1 in a debris layer 0.80 m above pavement level about 13 m to the northeast; B2 about 8 m north of B1 at the foot of a wall bordering the pavement
  • Now heldThe Israel Museum, Jerusalem (permanent display; Israel Antiquities Authority numbers 1993-3162 and 1996-125). Lent to the Jewish Museum, New York, 5 Dec 2024 - 5 Jan 2025, and to the Museum of the Bible, Washington DC, 30 Aug - 3 Nov 2025.
  • MaterialBasalt
  • DimensionsJoined fragments H 34 cm, W 32 cm (Israel Museum record). Langlois (2024) notes the original stele was under 50 cm wide.
  • ScriptOld Aramaic alphabet (Phoenician-type letter forms) with word dividers
  • LanguageOld Aramaic

Biblical Passages

  • 2 Kings 8:28-29; 9:14-28Joram of Israel and Ahaziah of Judah at war with Hazael at Ramoth-gilead, then both killed in Jehu's coup. The stele's restored names match these two kings, but in the stele the Aramaean king claims the killings, while Kings credits Jehu. The name match depends on the contested fragment placement.
  • 2 Kings 8:7-15Hazael takes the throne of Damascus after Ben-Hadad's death. The stele's speaker says his father 'lay down' and that Hadad made him king; if the speaker is Hazael, this is his own presentation of the succession (our reading).
  • 2 Kings 10:32-33Hazael 'cut Israel short' and struck Israel's territory in Jehu's days; the stele is an Aramaean boast of victories over Israel from the same arena (inference: the author is not named).
  • 1 Kings 15:20Ben-Hadad of Damascus strikes Dan: the town where the stele was set up was contested between Aram and Israel.
  • 2 Samuel 7:16; 1 Kings 12:19'House of David' as the name of the Judahite royal line. The stele uses bytdwd as the name of a kingdom/dynasty alongside 'king of Israel'; it does not mention David as an individual.
