Elephantine Papyri

Aramaic archives of the Judean garrison at Yeb (Elephantine), Egypt · 5th century BCE

Overview

Elephantine, an island in the Nile at the first cataract opposite Syene (Aswan), held a Persian-period military colony that included a Judean garrison. Its members wrote in Aramaic, the administrative language of the Persian empire. Their surviving papyri include marriage contracts, property deeds, loans, lists of contributions, private and official letters, and copies of literary works such as the Words of Ahiqar and a version of Darius I's Behistun inscription.

The best-known letter (Cowley 30) is a petition from Yedoniah and the priests of Yeb to Bigvai (Bagohi), 'governor of Judaea', dated the 20th of Marheshwan, year 17 of Darius. It reports that in year 14, while the satrap Arsames was away, the priests of the Egyptian god Khnum conspired with the local Persian official Waidrang (Vidranga), whose son Nephayan led troops who destroyed 'the temple of Ya'u the God' (YHW) in the fortress, burnt its cedar roof and took its gold and silver bowls. It says the temple already stood when Cambyses conquered Egypt (525 BCE), and that an earlier letter to Johanan the high priest in Jerusalem went unanswered. It also mentions a letter to Delaiah and Shelemiah, sons of Sanballat, governor of Samaria.

A short memorandum (Cowley 32) records the reply of Bigvai and Delaiah: the altar-house of the God of Heaven should be rebuilt 'as it was before', with meal-offering and incense offered on it. Burnt offering is not mentioned. Another letter (Cowley 21), from one Hananiah in year 5 of Darius, passes on a royal instruction about the festival of Unleavened Bread in Nisan (14th-21st). The word 'Passover' in it falls in a damaged part of the papyrus and is Cowley's restoration.

In its own words

To our lord Bigvai, governor of Judaea, your servants Yedoniah and his colleagues, the priests who are in Yeb the fortress... Already in the days of the kings of Egypt our fathers had built that temple in the fortress of Yeb, and when Cambyses came into Egypt he found that temple built.A. Cowley, Aramaic Papyri of the Fifth Century B.C. (Oxford, 1923), no. 30, lines 1 and 13-14, pp. 113-114

What the evidence shows

  • Cowley 30 (petition): dated '20th of Marheshwan, the 17th year of Darius the king'; recto lines 1-17, verso 18-30; Cowley calls it in excellent condition.
  • Names the god of the Yeb temple as Ya'u (YHW) and calls him 'God of Heaven'.
  • Temple destroyed in Tammuz, year 14 of Darius, by troops led by Nephayan on the order of his father Waidrang, at the urging of the priests of Khnum.
  • Temple details: stone pillars, five gateways of hewn stone, doors with bronze hinges, cedar roof, gold and silver basins.
  • Claims the temple existed before Cambyses entered Egypt and was spared when Egyptian temples were overthrown.
  • Names Johanan the high priest in Jerusalem, Ostanes brother of Anani, and Delaiah and Shelemiah, sons of Sanballat governor of Samaria.
  • Cowley 32: memorandum of Bigvai and Delaiah authorizing rebuilding 'as it was before' with meal-offering and incense; animal burnt offering is not listed.
  • Cowley 22 (contribution list), lines 123-125: the temple fund is divided between Ya'u and 'Anathbethel in nearly equal shares, with a smaller share to Ishumbethel; Cowley 44 records an oath 'by the temple and by 'Anathya'u' (Cowley 1923, Introduction).
  • Cowley 21: in year 5 of Darius, Hananiah conveys a royal instruction to keep days 15-21 of Nisan as Unleavened Bread; 'Passover' is restored in a lacuna.
  • Cowley 1923 notes that early critics (Belleli 1909, Jahn 1913) questioned authenticity over the double dates; Cowley rejected this after the astronomical studies of Knobel, Fotheringham and Smyly.

Open questions

  • How 'Jewish' was the Yeb community's religion? The texts show a YHW temple with sacrifice outside Jerusalem, contrary to Deut 12's single-sanctuary law. The archive's own contribution list (Cowley 22) divides the temple fund between Ya'u, 'Anathbethel and Ishumbethel, and an oath is sworn 'by 'Anathya'u' (Cowley 44); Cowley (1923) concluded they recognized 'Anath, Bethel, Ishum and Herem besides Ya'u, while admitting 'the evidence does not show' how these related. J. D. Moore ('A Supposed Diaspora Community at Elephantine or How the Persians Reinvented Judean-ness', in Judeans in the Land of the Nile, Zaphon 2026) questions the 'diaspora' label itself. G. Granerød in the same volume weighs Papyrus Amherst 63 as evidence for the community's background. What the papyri do not say is where or when the community's ancestors left Judah. Moore's 2026 monograph goes further, treating the colony as 'colonial state dependents' of the Persian administration rather than mercenaries.
  • Language of the community: M. A. Cunningham ('A Case for Hebrew at Yeb: The Vidranga Passage and Judean Diglossia', JNES 84/1, 2025) argues from the wording of the Vidranga passage in the temple petition that Hebrew was still in use alongside Aramaic. The documents themselves are all Aramaic.
  • Was the 'Passover Papyrus' about Passover? Cowley (1923, no. 21) restores 'Passover' in damaged lines. The surviving text clearly concerns the seven days of Unleavened Bread, Nisan 15-21, by royal instruction. How far the restoration can be trusted is a question about the gap, not something the preserved text settles.
  • Why was burnt offering left out of the rebuilding memorandum (Cowley 32)? The document gives no reason. Cowley and later writers connect it to Egyptian (Khnum-priest) hostility to animal sacrifice or to Jerusalem's claims; that is inference from silence.

Since 2024

  • 2025-04M. A. Cunningham argued in JNES that the Vidranga passage of the temple petition points to Hebrew-Aramaic diglossia among the Judeans of Yeb. [3]

The Find

  • Date5th century BCE, mostly the reigns of Artaxerxes I and Darius II; the dated temple petition (Cowley 30) is year 17 of Darius II (Cowley: 408 BCE); the festival letter (Cowley 21) is year 5 of Darius II (Cowley: 419 BCE)
  • How datedRegnal-year dates written in the documents themselves (often double-dated by Babylonian/Jewish and Egyptian months). Cowley identifies the Darius of nos. 21 and 30 as Darius II because the same community leader, Yedoniah, appears in both.
  • DiscoveredPapyri surfaced on the antiquities market from the 1890s; the Sayce-Cowley group was published 1906; German excavations on Elephantine in 1906-1908 recovered the papyri published by Sachau (1911), Groups acquired or excavated by various parties; editions by A. H. Sayce and A. E. Cowley (1906), E. Sachau (1911), A. E. Cowley (collected edition, 1923), E. G. Kraeling (Brooklyn papyri, 1953), Elephantine island (ancient Yeb) at Aswan (Syene), southern Egypt
  • Now heldDispersed. Cowley (1923) names Cairo and Berlin as holding the originals of the major groups; the Brooklyn Museum holds the papyri published by Kraeling in 1953; other pieces are in other collections. Individual inventory numbers were Not confirmed in a published museum record.
  • MaterialPapyrus (letters, contracts, lists, literary texts such as Ahiqar)
  • ScriptAramaic (square-type cursive of the Achaemenid chancery)
  • LanguageImperial (Official) Aramaic

Biblical Passages

  • Neh 2:10, 19; 4:1-2; 13:28Sanballat appears in Nehemiah as Nehemiah's opponent. Cowley 30 names 'Delaiah and Shelemiah the sons of Sanballat governor of Samaria' in the 17th year of Darius II: an outside document naming Sanballat as governor of Samaria and giving his sons' names. Cowley uses this and Bigvai's office in 408 to put Nehemiah's 32nd year of Artaxerxes (Neh 13:6) under Artaxerxes I, 433 BCE.
  • Neh 12:22-23Lists Johanan among high priests of the Persian period. Cowley 30 names 'Johanan the high priest' in Jerusalem in Darius II's reign.
  • Ezra 4-7; Ezra 5:11-12Aramaic chancery letters in Ezra use the same forms and title 'God of Heaven' as the papyri. The petition's pattern (appeal to Persian officials to rebuild a YHW temple, with a written authorization in reply) is the same process Ezra 5-6 describes for Jerusalem.
  • Exod 12:15-20; Lev 23:5-8Cowley 21's Nisan 14 / Nisan 15-21 dates, no leaven seen in dwellings, and no work on the 15th and 21st correspond to the Torah's festival rules.
  • Jer 44:1Jeremiah addresses Judeans living in Egypt 'at Migdol, Tahpanhes, Noph and Pathros'. Elephantine is in the far south (Pathros); the papyri show a Judean community there a century later. Any direct link between the two groups is not in the sources.
