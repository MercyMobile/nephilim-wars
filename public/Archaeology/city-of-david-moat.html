Rock-cut moat between the City of David and the Ophel

Rock-cut ditch dividing the lower city from the acropolis · in use by the Late Iron IIA, backfilled in the Late Hellenistic period

Overview

Excavations in the Givati Parking Lot, on the ridge south of the Ophel and Temple Mount, exposed a man-made rock-cut ditch close to the summit of the hill, bounded on both north and south by near-vertical scarps, with an installation of intertwined channels in it. Yuval Gadot, Efrat Bocher, Liora Freud and Yiftah Shalev published it in Tel Aviv 50 (2023) as 'An Early Iron Age Moat in Jerusalem between the Ophel and the Southeastern Ridge/City of David.'

By comparing it with Kathleen Kenyon's 1960s excavation slightly to the east, where she had recorded bedrock dropping to the north where it should rise, the team concluded that one ditch crossed the whole width of the ridge and cut the residential lower city off from the acropolis to the north. The July 2024 announcement gave the joined length as at least 70 m west-east.

The date when the ditch was first cut is not established. The published abstract states it served as a moat by the Late Iron IIA and stayed open until Late Hellenistic construction filled it in. Shalev's 2024 statement adds that Middle Bronze Age cutting is possible because major quarrying in Jerusalem is usually of that date, and that the excavators are 'confident that it was used at the time of the First Temple and the Kingdom of Judah (ninth century BCE)'.

What the evidence shows

  • Man-made ditch about 30 m wide and at least 6 m deep near the summit of the Southeastern Ridge, with rock scarps on its north and south sides (Gadot et al., Tel Aviv 50, 2023, abstract).
  • An installation of intertwined channels lies in the ditch; its function is described as 'enigmatic' (Gadot et al. 2023, abstract).
  • Joined with Kenyon's sloping-bedrock observation farther east, the ditch crosses the full width of the ridge, separating the Southeastern Ridge from the Ophel (Gadot et al. 2023, abstract; City of David Foundation release).
  • Press figures: at least 9 m deep, at least 30 m wide, at least 70 m west-east (City of David Foundation release; Times of Israel, 21 July 2024).
  • The ditch served as a moat by the Late Iron IIA and was backfilled by Late Hellenistic construction (Gadot et al. 2023, abstract).
  • The date of first cutting 'cannot be determined' (Gadot et al. 2023) / 'is unknown' (Shalev, 2024 release).

Open questions

  • When it was cut. Gadot, Bocher, Freud and Shalev (Tel Aviv 50, 2023) title the paper 'An Early Iron Age Moat' but state that 'the initial cutting of the ditch cannot be determined'; the fixed point is its use by the Late Iron IIA. Shalev (IAA/City of David release, 2024) raises a Middle Bronze Age cutting because, in his words, such large construction and quarrying works in Jerusalem 'are usually dated to the Middle Bronze Age'. No MB material from the cut itself is reported in the sources read. The full paper is not openly accessible (publisher access restriction), so the ceramic basis of 'Late Iron IIA' is known here only from the abstract.
  • Whether this is the Millo or part of it. Gadot (2024 release) says the find 'opens up a renewed discussion' of the terms Ophel and Millo; the release quotes 1 Kgs 11:27. Neither the paper abstract nor the release identifies the moat as the Millo. 'Millo' comes from the root 'fill', and the moat was a cut, later filled only in the Hellenistic period; our reading is that a direct equation needs an argument the sources read here do not make.
  • Depth: the 2023 paper gives 'at least 6 m', the 2024 release 'at least nine meters'. Whether this reflects further excavation after the paper went to press or a different measuring point is not stated in either source.

The Find

  • DateCutting date unknown. In use as a moat by the Late Iron IIA (the excavators' press statement: 9th century BCE) and until the Late Hellenistic period, when it was backfilled
  • How datedStratigraphy and pottery in the fills against the scarps, as summarized in the Tel Aviv 50 (2023) abstract: 'Although the initial cutting of the ditch cannot be determined, it is clear that by the Late Iron IIA, the ditch served as a moat.' A Middle Bronze Age cutting is a suggestion by analogy with other large quarrying works in Jerusalem, not a dated observation (Shalev, quoted by the City of David Foundation).
  • DiscoveredGivati Parking Lot excavations; first published 2023, announced to the press July 2024, Israel Antiquities Authority and Tel Aviv University; directors Yuval Gadot (TAU) and Yiftah Shalev (IAA); funded by the City of David Foundation, Givati Parking Lot, north-western part of the City of David (Southeastern Ridge), Jerusalem Walls National Park; linked to a sloping rock surface exposed farther east by Kathleen Kenyon in the 1960s
  • Now heldIn situ, Givati Parking Lot excavation, City of David, Jerusalem
  • MaterialCut in bedrock, with vertical rock scarps on north and south
  • DimensionsTel Aviv 2023 paper: about 30 m wide, at least 6 m deep. 2024 press release: at least 9 m deep, at least 30 m wide, traced at least 70 m west-east when joined to Kenyon's section

Biblical Passages

  • 2 Samuel 5:7-9David takes the stronghold of Zion, calls it the City of David, and 'built round about from Millo and inward' (KJV). The moat marks a physical northern edge of the lower city in the Iron Age; whether it existed in David's day is not established, since the cutting date is unknown.
  • 1 Kings 9:15, 24; 11:27Solomon builds the Millo and 'repaired the breaches of the city of David his father' (KJV, 11:27). The excavators cite this verse as the kind of topographic reshaping the moat represents; they do not identify the moat as the Millo.
  • 2 Chronicles 27:3; 33:14; Nehemiah 3:26-27Jotham and Manasseh build on 'the wall of Ophel'; Nehemiah's builders repair toward 'the wall of Ophel'. The find physically separates the Ophel from the ridge to the south, giving a concrete boundary to the term; the texts do not mention a moat.
