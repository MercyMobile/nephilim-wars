Tell el-Dab'a (Avaris): the Levantine Town in the Eastern Delta

Excavated Delta town settled by Western Asiatic immigrants from the late 12th Dynasty, later the Hyksos capital · tooth-enamel isotopes show about half of 75 sampled people grew up outside the Delta

Overview

Tell el-Dab'a is the site of ancient Avaris in the eastern Nile Delta. It began as a planned Egyptian settlement at the start of the 12th Dynasty. Manfred Bietak, who directed 75 excavation campaigns there between 1966 and 2011, reports that during the late 12th Dynasty people he calls Canaanites moved into the Egyptian town. They built house types foreign to Egypt (the middle-room house, the broad-room house and the hearth-house) and buried their dead with Near Eastern weapons and with donkeys and goats (Bietak 2022).

The community stayed and grew. Two large mansions stood at the start of the 13th Dynasty, with a cemetery of dignitaries in their gardens; later their statues were smashed and the mansions abandoned. One phase ends with many emergency burials, which Bietak reads as epidemic or violence. A precinct of Near Eastern temples was built about 1720 BCE. The town then became the capital of the Hyksos, the foreign 15th Dynasty, covering some 620–640 acres, until Ahmose I took it about 1530 BCE. Its southern part was later absorbed into Piramesse, the Delta residence of Ramesses II (Bietak 2022).

Three laboratory studies of the excavated skeletons add a second kind of evidence. Strontium in tooth enamel shows that 40 of 75 sampled people (53%) spent childhood outside the Nile Delta, with more non-locals before Hyksos rule than during it (Stantis et al. 2020). Adding oxygen isotopes raises the count to 45 of 75 (Stantis et al. 2021). Dental traits of 90 people set Avaris apart from other Egyptian sites, and show the Delta-born and the immigrants to be one population (Maaranen et al. 2021).

What the evidence shows

  • Late 12th Dynasty: a settlement of Canaanites with middle-room, broad-room and hearth-houses, and a cemetery of weapon-bearers and high dignitaries (Bietak 2022, fig. 4, after Bietak 1996, fig. 7).
  • Tombs with donkey and goat burials and prestigious Near Eastern weapons; a scarab with the title 'Ruler of Retjenu', which Bietak takes to show an Egyptian-appointed head of the community (Bietak 2022).
  • Early 13th Dynasty: two mansions at area F/I, one growing to 2,400 m², with a dignitaries' cemetery in the gardens; a statue of a Western Asiatic dignitary, now Munich ÄS 7171 (Bietak 2022).
  • Phase G ends with numerous emergency burials, mostly without offerings, in areas A/II and F/I (Bietak 2022).
  • Sacred precinct in the Eastern Town (A/II): Near Eastern temple III, 32.5 × 21.5 m, dated by Bietak to the time of Nehesy, c. 1720 BCE (Bietak 2022).
  • Strontium (87Sr/86Sr) in tooth enamel of 75 individuals: 40 (53%) non-local to the Delta; 36 individuals pre-Hyksos and 35 Hyksos-period; significantly more non-locals in the pre-Hyksos group (t(70) = 2.6, p = 0.012); 77% of the females non-local (Stantis et al. 2020).
  • With oxygen isotopes added, 45 of the same 75 (60%) are classed non-local; no clear dietary difference between immigrants and locals; first-generation immigrants and Delta-born alike were buried with elite Asiatic customs (Stantis et al. 2021).
  • Dental non-metric traits of 90 individuals: Avaris differs significantly (p < 0.01) from other Egyptian samples; the 30 'locals' and 33 'non-locals' of the isotope study are not ancestrally distinct from one another (Maaranen et al. 2021).
  • Ahmose I took Avaris c. 1530 BCE, on the evidence of the tomb inscription of Ahmose son of Ibana; most of the town was then abandoned (Bietak 2022).

Open questions

  • Where did the settlers come from? Stantis et al. (2020) found the non-local strontium values so varied that the incomers 'were not from a unified place of origin', and could not name the regions. Bietak (2022) calls the first settlers Canaanites. In a 2024 preprint Bietak ties the early elite to the northern Levant, Byblos in particular, sets aside a southern Levantine origin, and proposes that the people behind Hyksos rule came from Asiatic communities already living elsewhere in Egypt, near the Memphis–Fayum region. These three accounts do not agree with one another.
  • Invasion or rise from within? Josephus, quoting Manetho, tells of an invasion. Stantis et al. (2020) read the isotopes as an immigrant community that 'gained power' in a city it already knew. Bietak's 2024 preprint reports that the palace assigned to the 14th Dynasty was burned, and that violence and turmoil attended the change to Hyksos rule. An internal rise and a violent one are not exclusive.
  • What has this to do with Israel in Egypt? No inscription from the site names Jacob, Joseph, Israel or Hebrews, and Bietak's 2022 entry does not raise the subject. The town is in the region where Genesis puts Jacob's family, and its Levantine community begins in the late 12th Dynasty. Whether that is before, during or after the biblical sojourn depends on the chronology adopted for both Egypt and Genesis; on the conventional Egyptian dates, where the 12th Dynasty ends about 1800 BCE, and a reckoning that brings Jacob to Egypt about 1700 BCE, the Levantine town was already about a century old.

The Find

  • DateEgyptian planned settlement from the start of the 12th Dynasty; Western Asiatic community from the late 12th Dynasty onward; capital of the Hyksos (15th Dynasty); taken by Ahmose I c. 1530 BCE (Bietak 2022)
  • How datedExcavation stratigraphy tied to the Egyptian dynasties through pottery, scarabs and royal names (Bietak 2022, with its stratigraphic chart); the end date rests on the tomb inscription of the naval officer Ahmose son of Ibana at El-Kab. The isotope and dental studies sort individuals into pre-Hyksos and Hyksos-period groups by the excavators' phasing.
  • DiscoveredÉdouard Naville 1895; Labib Habachi in the 1940s (he proposed the identification with Avaris in 1941–42); Shehata Adam at 'Ezbet Rushdi in the 1950s; Austrian excavations under Manfred Bietak 1966–2011 (75 campaigns); Irene Forstner-Müller at 'Ezbet Rushdi 2010–15, Austrian Archaeological Institute and University of Vienna (Manfred Bietak, then Irene Forstner-Müller), after Naville, Habachi and Adam, Tell el-Dab'a and 'Ezbet Rushdi, eastern Nile Delta, Egypt
  • Now heldIn situ, Tell el-Dab'a, eastern Nile Delta, Egypt
  • MaterialMudbrick town: houses, mansions, a palace and temples; cemeteries with human, donkey and goat burials; pottery, scarabs, weapons and statuary
  • DimensionsThe town grew from about 240 to about 620 acres; Bietak (2022) also gives 640 acres for the Hyksos period

Biblical Passages

  • Genesis 46:28–47:6, 27Jacob's family settles in the eastern Delta as herdsmen, keeps its identity and multiplies. Avaris shows a West Asian community doing that in the eastern Delta over generations: its own houses, its own burial customs, intermarriage and growth. The fit is of setting. No find names the family, and the town was not founded by a single household.
  • Genesis 47:11; Exodus 1:11; 12:37'The land of Rameses' and the store-city Raamses. Bietak identifies Avaris as the southern part of Piramesse, the Delta residence of Ramesses II, so the same ground carries both the Middle Bronze Age Levantine town and the Ramesside city (Bietak 2022).
  • Genesis 41:41–45; 50:26Joseph as a Semite in high Egyptian office. The 13th-Dynasty mansions with their cemetery of dignitaries, the statue of a Western Asiatic official and the 'Ruler of Retjenu' scarab show West Asians holding rank in this town. None is identified with Joseph by the excavator in the sources read here.
  • Genesis 12:10; 42:1–5People of Canaan going down to Egypt. Compare the 37 Asiatics registered at Beni Hasan in the same dynasty.
