Jerubba'al inscription, Khirbet al-Ra'i

Proto-Canaanite ink inscription on a small jug · late 12th - early 11th century BCE

Overview

Three small sherds of one painted and burnished closed vessel, perhaps a jug, carry letters written in brown ink in Proto-Canaanite (early alphabetic) script. Two were found on the last day of the 2019 season in the sifted fill of a stone-lined silo (A310) in Area A at Khirbet al-Ra'i, a 1.7-hectare site 4 km west of Lachish; the third turned up when the whole silo was emptied and sifted in 2020. The silo had become a dump for household rubbish in an earlier Iron Age I phase.

Two sherds join exactly and preserve five letters. The editors read the second to fifth as resh, bet, ayin (with the dot 'pupil' of the old eye-sign) and lamed; the first is only partly preserved and is 'most consistent' with a yod. Read right to left this gives Yrb'l, the personal name Jerubba'al. Traces of a sixth letter on the joined sherds and of two letters on the third sherd cannot be read.

The editio princeps (Rollston, Garfinkel, Keimer, Davis and Ganor, Jerusalem Journal of Archaeology 2, 2021) notes that Jerubba'al is the name given to Gideon in Judges 6:32, but states that no patronymic is present and that an identification with the biblical figure would need a patronymic, title or epithet. They date the context to the late 12th or early 11th century BCE and present it as the first Iron Age I inscription from the Shephelah, bridging the Late Bronze Age Lachish texts and the 10th-century Qeiyafa and Beth-Shemesh texts.

What the text says (summary)

[y]rb'l -- 'Jerubba'al' (the first letter partly preserved, read as yod)Our transliteration and rendering of the reading in Rollston et al., Jerusalem Journal of Archaeology 2 (2021): 8, 12. No public-domain translation exists (found 2019).

What the evidence shows

  • Found in Silo A310, Area A, Khirbet al-Ra'i: two sherds on the last day of the 2019 season, a third in 2020 when the whole silo was sifted (Rollston et al. 2021: 3-4).
  • All three sherds belong to one small closed vessel, perhaps a jug, painted, burnished and fired; no other joining sherds were found in the probe (Rollston et al. 2021: 4).
  • Brown ink; eight letters or traces in total on the three sherds; five decipherable on the two joined sherds (Rollston et al. 2021: 8).
  • Letters 2-5 are clear: resh, bet, ayin, lamed. Letter 1 is incomplete; the editors prefer yod but name aleph and ed (dh) as possible alternatives (Rollston et al. 2021: 8, 12).
  • The ayin retains its internal dot, and the bet stands rotated 90 degrees from its Phoenician stance, features the editors use to classify the script as Proto-Canaanite rather than Phoenician or Old Hebrew (Rollston et al. 2021: 11).
  • Direction of writing is argued, not given: read left to right the letters could yield l- plus 'bry; the editors prefer right to left because of the stance of the resh (Rollston et al. 2021: 11-12).
  • No patronymic is present (Rollston et al. 2021: 9).
  • Context date: pottery of the second half of the 12th and the 11th century BCE, stratigraphically below a level radiocarbon-dated to c. 1050 BCE, giving c. 1150-1050 BCE (Rollston et al. 2021: 6-7).
  • The silo fill was domestic rubbish of a few hundred sherds, no complete vessels (Rollston et al. 2021: 4, 6).

Open questions

  • Is the first letter a yod? Rollston et al. (JJAR 2, 2021: 12) read the partly preserved first sign as yod, comparing the Lachish ewer, but state the traces could be aleph (giving a Ba'al name with the root nwr, 'light') or ed (dh). The reading Jerubba'al depends on this one incomplete letter.
  • Which direction does it read? The editors (2021: 11-12) prefer right to left because of the stance of the resh; they allow that left to right could read as a lamed plus 'bry, a name or ethnic term. Proto-Canaanite of this period was not fixed in direction (they compare the left-to-right Qubur Walaydah lamed).
  • Is this Gideon? The editors (2021: 9) say it 'would be tempting' but, following Mykytiuk (2004: 32), an identification needs a patronymic, title or epithet, and none is present; they note Gideon is placed at Ophrah of Manasseh (Judg 6:11), 'not the same region' though 'not that distant'. Keimer, Davis, Ganor and Garfinkel (Biblical Archaeology Review, Winter 2025, as summarised by BAS, Feb 2026) again treat it as evidence of the name in the period, not of the biblical man. No publication located argues the vessel was Gideon's.
  • What does the name mean? The editors (2021: 9-10) follow Noth (1928) in deriving it from rby/rbb with Ba'al, 'May Ba'al be great', and note that Judges 6:32 gives a folk etymology from ryb, 'let Ba'al contend'. They add that ba'al ('lord') could early on refer to Yahweh (e.g. the name Bealiah, 1 Chr 12:5/6). This is the editors' reading of the name, not something the three sherds decide.

Corrected from the earlier edition of this page
  • Headlines presenting it as 'evidence of Gideon' (e.g. CBN 'Israeli Archaeologists Uncover Evidence of Gideon', Dec 2022, seen in search results) go beyond the editors, who state no patronymic is present and an identification cannot be made (Rollston et al. 2021: 9; Garfinkel: 'we cannot tell whether he owned the vessel', Times of Israel 2021).
  • Press describing a 'five-letter inscription' on 'three sherds' (Times of Israel 2021) conflates two things: the five letters are on the two joined sherds; the three sherds together carry eight letters or traces, three of them unreadable (Rollston et al. 2021: 8).
  • The name is often given as fully legible. The first letter is only partly preserved; yod is the editors' preferred reading over aleph or ed (Rollston et al. 2021: 12).
  • Claims that it is 'the first inscription to be found from the time of the Judges' (e.g. ABR, Wood 2022; ynet 2021 'first-ever inscription from time of biblical judges', seen in search results) are broader than the editio princeps, which says only that no Iron Age I inscription had previously been found in the Shephelah, and itself cites the Qubur Walaydah inscription for comparison (Rollston et al. 2021: 11-12).
  • The meaning is often given as 'let Baal contend'. That is the folk etymology in Judges 6:32; the editors derive the name, with Noth, from rby/rbb, 'May Ba'al be great' (Rollston et al. 2021: 9-10).

The Find

  • DateLate 12th or first half of the 11th century BCE (c. 1150-1050 BCE)
  • How datedArchaeological context: fill of stone-lined Silo A310 in an earlier Iron Age I phase of Area A, dated by its pottery (bell-shaped bowls absent from Lachish Level VI; CP-5 cooking pot rim) to the second half of the 12th and the 11th century BCE, and lying stratigraphically below the upper Iron Age I level radiometrically dated to c. 1050 BCE. The editors state the palaeographic date agrees (Rollston et al. 2021: 6-8, 12).
  • DiscoveredTwo sherds on the last day of the 2019 season; a third sherd in the 2020 season, Khirbet al-Ra'i expedition (Yosef Garfinkel, Hebrew University; Saar Ganor, Israel Antiquities Authority; Kyle H. Keimer and Gil Davis, Macquarie University); read by Christopher Rollston, Khirbet al-Ra'i, 4 km west of Tel Lachish, Judean Shephelah: Area A, probe in the south-eastern side, sifted fill of Silo A310
  • Now heldIsrael Antiquities Authority (photographed by Dafna Gazit, IAA); no museum or inventory number located
  • MaterialBrown ink on pottery: three sherds of one small closed vessel, perhaps a jug, painted, burnished and kiln-fired
  • ScriptProto-Canaanite (Early Alphabetic), probably written right-to-left
  • LanguageNorthwest Semitic (a personal name)

Biblical Passages

  • Judges 6:31-32Joash says 'Let Baal plead against him'; 'Therefore on that day he called him Jerubbaal' (KJV). The sherds carry the same consonants, yrb'l, from the same general period (12th-11th c. BCE). The inscription has no patronymic, so it attests the name, not the man.
  • Judges 7:1; 8:29, 35; 9:1-57Further uses of Jerubbaal as Gideon's name. The inscription shows the name was in real use in southern Canaan in Iron Age I; its findspot is in the Shephelah, while Gideon's Ophrah is in Manasseh (Judg 6:11).
  • 2 Samuel 11:21The name altered to Jerubbesheth, with 'shame' replacing Ba'al. The editors cite this as the later pejorative treatment of Ba'al names; the inscription preserves the unaltered form.
