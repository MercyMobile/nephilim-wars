Alexamenos Graffito

Scratched drawing of a donkey-headed figure on a cross with a Greek caption, from the imperial Paedagogium on the Palatine · about 200 CE

Overview

The graffito was scratched into the plaster of a room in the Paedagogium, a dependency of the imperial palace thought to have housed or trained the emperor's household slaves. It shows a figure with a donkey's head on a cross, seen from behind, and a man at the left raising a hand toward it. A Greek caption runs beneath.

The museum reads the caption as 'Alexamenos worships [his] god' and describes the drawing as hard to interpret, probably a mockery of a Christian. If so, it is among the earliest pictures of a crucifixion, and it is drawn by someone who thought the worship absurd.

The insult is not isolated. Christian writers of the same generation report the charge that Christians worshipped a donkey's head: Tertullian in the Apology and To the Nations, and Minucius Felix, who gives it to the pagan speaker of his dialogue along with scorn for the worship of a crucified man and his cross.

What the evidence shows

  • Findspot and date: discovered 1857 in the Paedagogium on the Palatine; dated to the Severan age (Parco archeologico del Colosseo catalogue).
  • The drawing: a donkey-headed figure on a cross seen from behind, a man at the left with raised arm, and a Y-shaped sign at the upper right (museum catalogue).
  • The caption is written across four lines (museum catalogue); 33.5 × 38 cm, plaster (Judaism and Rome database).
  • Standard publication: H. Solin and M. Itkonen-Kaila, Graffiti del Palatino I: Paedagogium (1966), no. 246. The building yielded 369 graffiti in Latin and Greek (Judaism and Rome database, citing Solin and Itkonen-Kaila).
  • The donkey-head charge in contemporary writers: Tertullian, Apology 16 and To the Nations 1.14; Minucius Felix, Octavius 9, where the pagan speaker says Christians worship a crucified man, his cross, and the head of an ass (as quoted by J. Grout and the Catholic Encyclopedia).
  • Tertullian also describes a picture carried about in Carthage, a figure in a toga with a donkey's ears and a hoof, labelled as the god of the Christians (To the Nations 1.14, as quoted by Grout).

Open questions

  • Is the target a Christian? The museum calls that probable, not certain. The literary evidence for the donkey slander supports it. R. Wünsch proposed instead a Gnostic group that identified Christ with the donkey-headed Seth-Typhon; the Catholic Encyclopedia (1907) reports this and judges the reasons unconvincing.
  • The Y sign. The museum reports two readings, a cry of pain or a pitchfork. D. Balch and others doubt that the upsilon belongs to the original graffito (Judaism and Rome database).
  • The grammar. The verb as written does not agree with its subject; O. L. Yarbrough reads σέβετε as a phonetic spelling of σέβεται, 'worships' (Judaism and Rome database).
  • A reply? Secondary accounts report a second graffito in a neighbouring room reading 'Alexamenos fidelis'. This was not checked against Solin and Itkonen-Kaila and is not used here as evidence.
  • The date. 'About 200' is the museum's Severan date for the graffito in its setting. It is an estimate.

Biblical Passages
