Ipuwer Papyrus

Egyptian lament of a land turned upside down · New Kingdom copy of an older poem

Overview

A long hieratic papyrus in Leiden, bought in 1828 from the collection of Giovanni Anastasi, who said it came from Memphis. The front (recto) holds a literary poem in seventeen pages, with the beginning lost and pages 8-17 badly damaged. The back holds sun hymns in a different, later hand. Alan H. Gardiner published the text and translation in 1909. He measured the roll at 378 cm long and 18 cm high.

The speaker is a sage named Ipuwer. He laments a land in collapse: civil strife, plundered tombs and offices, servants taking their masters' place, foreigners ('Asiatics') in the Delta, trade with Byblos and Keftiu cut off, famine, death, and a river that 'is blood'. The latter part addresses a 'Lord of All', and the final pages are fragmentary. Most of the poem is built from refrains repeated line after line ('Forsooth...', 'Behold...', 'Destroyed is...').

Modern readers who connect it with the Exodus point to the blood-river line and the general picture of catastrophe. Egyptologists connect it with a period of internal breakdown in Egypt, or read it as a literary lament. The manuscript itself names no king, no Hebrews and no Moses, has no plague sequence, and carries no internal date.

In its own words

Forsooth, (men's) hearts are violent. Plague is throughout the land. Blood is everywhere. … Forsooth, the river is blood, and (yet) men drink of it. Men shrink from (?) (tasting) human beings, and thirst after water.A. H. Gardiner, The Admonitions of an Egyptian Sage from a Hieratic Papyrus in Leiden (Pap. Leiden 344 recto) (Leipzig: Hinrichs, 1909), translation of Admonitions 2,5-6 and 2,10 (page.line of the papyrus). Gardiner notes that the word he renders 'plague' in 2,5 is the word the first Sallier papyrus uses of the Hyksos.

What the evidence shows

  • Object: Papyrus Leiden I 344, 378 cm × 18 cm, inscribed on both sides. The recto has 17 pages of about 14 lines each (Gardiner 1909, Introduction §1).
  • The recto's hand is dated no earlier than the start of the 19th Dynasty. Unfilled spaces and omissions suggest the scribe was copying from a damaged older manuscript (Gardiner 1909, Introduction §1).
  • Acquired at the 1828 Anastasi sale. Anastasi reported Memphis as the source, and there is no excavated context.
  • The text says 'the river is blood, and (yet) men drink of it' (2,10) and 'Plague is throughout the land. Blood is everywhere' (2,5-6).
  • Asiatics are in the Delta: 'A foreign tribe from abroad has come to Egypt' (3,1) and 'The Asiatics are skilled in the arts of the Marshlands' (4,5-8) (Gardiner's summary translation).
  • Social reversal: gold, lapis lazuli, silver and malachite 'are fastened on the necks of female slaves' (3,2-3), and 'He who had no grain is (now) the possessor of granaries' (9,4-5).
  • Cattle: 'Cattle moan because of the state of the land' (5,5), and 'cattle are left to stray, and there is none to gather them together' (9,2-3).
  • The text names no pharaoh, no Israelites and no event that can be dated. The beginning of the poem is lost.

Open questions

  • When was it composed? A. H. Gardiner (1909, Appendix) weighed two backgrounds: the First Intermediate Period (6th-11th Dynasties) and the Hyksos period (K. Sethe). He came down for the 12th Dynasty, citing the language and administrative details such as the six 'Great Houses' (6,12), 'but … we have no means of attaining anything more than a strong presumptive probability.' J. Van Seters (JEA 50, 1964) dated it to the late Middle Kingdom and Second Intermediate Period from its vocabulary, its administrative titles and its references to Asiatics. R. Enmarch (2009) treats it as a Middle Kingdom literary composition (c. 1980-1630 BCE) and reads it as literature rather than reportage.
  • Is it an Egyptian account of the Exodus plagues? I. Velikovsky (Ages in Chaos, 1952) read it as an Egyptian witness to the plagues and moved the Exodus to the end of the Middle Kingdom, starting from parallels such as the blood-river line. (His chapter has not been opened for this entry; this summary of his claim comes from secondary accounts.) Against that reading, from the text itself: it has no sequence of plagues, no Hebrews, and no slave people leaving. The foreign Asiatics it names are entering Egypt, not leaving it. Gardiner tied the 'plague' word in 2,5 to the Hyksos by way of the Sallier papyrus. Any correspondence with Exodus is a reading brought to the text from outside.
  • History or literature? A reading of the text as a record of real conditions (Gardiner 1909, who thought it described a real collapse) competes with a reading of it as a literary lament using stock 'world turned upside-down' themes (Enmarch 2009). The papyrus contains no dated event that could decide between them.

The surviving manuscript is a New Kingdom copy whose hand Gardiner (1909) dates to no earlier than the start of the 19th Dynasty. The date of composition is disputed (12th Dynasty per Gardiner; Second Intermediate Period per Van Seters 1964) and is not fixed at the 13th Dynasty.

  • The old entry said the text describes 'the suffering of the poor, the plundering of the wealthy'. It mostly describes the reverse: the poor become rich and servants take their masters' places (Gardiner's section on the social order reversed).
  • The old entry said the papyrus reveals conditions 'that may have contributed to... the Israelites' enslavement and subsequent liberation'. The text never mentions Israelites, slaves leaving Egypt or any plague sequence. The foreigners it names are Asiatics entering the Delta.
  • The old entry said 'many scholars draw parallels'. The specific parallel reading is Velikovsky's (1952). The shared material is one image, a blood-river, plus general disaster themes.

The Find

  • DateCopy: 19th Dynasty (Gardiner 1909: handwriting 'impossible' to put earlier than the beginning of the 19th Dynasty), i.e. 13th century BCE on the conventional chronology. Composition: disputed, somewhere between the First Intermediate Period and the Second Intermediate Period.
  • How datedPalaeography dates the copy: Gardiner (1909, Introduction §1) puts the hand of the recto no earlier than the start of the 19th Dynasty and notes signs of an older exemplar. Language and content are the only evidence for the date of composition, and the text has no internal regnal date.
  • DiscoveredBefore 1828 (bought at the Anastasi sale in 1828), Giovanni Anastasi (collector); purchased for the Leiden museum, Memphis according to Anastasi, which Gardiner understood as Saqqara. The exact findspot is unrecorded.
  • Now heldRijksmuseum van Oudheden, Leiden, Netherlands: Papyrus Leiden I 344 (recto = Ipuwer; verso = hymns to a sun god)
  • MaterialPapyrus, inscribed on both sides; now mounted in book form under varnish, which darkened it
  • Dimensions378 cm long in its present damaged state; 18 cm high (Gardiner 1909, Introduction §1)
  • ScriptHieratic, in a 'small and crabbed literary hand' (Gardiner)
  • LanguageMiddle Egyptian

Biblical Passages

  • Exod 7:17-21The river turned to blood: the Nile's water 'shall be turned to blood … and the Egyptians shall lothe to drink of the water of the river' (KJV v.18). Ipuwer 2,10: 'the river is blood, and (yet) men drink of it … thirst after water'. Both passages share the image of a blood-river people cannot drink. Ipuwer attaches it to no plague sequence, no staff and no Moses.
  • Exod 9:3-6Livestock afflicted. Ipuwer has cattle straying and moaning, but it does not have them dying by a stroke from God.
  • Exod 12:35-36Jewellery of gold and silver passing to servants. Ipuwer 3,2-3 has gold, silver and lapis lazuli 'on the necks of female slaves'. The direction differs: in Exodus Egyptians give to departing Israelites; in Ipuwer Egyptian servants take their masters' wealth.
  • Ps 78:44; 105:29Later retellings of the rivers turned to blood. They share the same single image only.
