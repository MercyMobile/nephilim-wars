Magdala Synagogue

Early Roman synagogue with the carved 'Magdala Stone' · 1st century CE

Overview

Between 2009 and 2013 the Israel Antiquities Authority excavated the northern quarter of Migdal ahead of hotel construction and exposed dwellings, a stone-paved street, an industrial quarter with small pools, and a synagogue north of the road at its western end. The excavators identify the site with Migdal Nunia / Taricheae, the chief town of the western shore before Tiberias was founded in 19 CE.

The latest synagogue phase, preserved as abandoned, had a long vestibule, a reading room surrounded by a raised corridor with stone benches, and a small side room. Two stylobates in the reading room carried basalt columns (E. Netzer suggested six). Walls and columns bore colored fresco panels (dark red, mustard yellow, blue); a mosaic with a rosette flanked by meanders was begun in the corridor and apparently left unfinished.

On a bed of small stones in the reading room lay a four-legged rectangular stone block carved in relief on five sides. One side shows a seven-branched menorah on a triangular base standing on a square structure, flanked by amphorae and columns. The excavators read the stone as representing the Second Temple and used as a prayer table.

What the evidence shows

  • Three construction phases; the earliest, mid-1st century BCE, 'probably not used as a synagogue' (HA-ESI 125).
  • Coin of 43 CE sealed in the foundation of the reading-room mosaic - a terminus post quem for that floor.
  • Coin of 80 CE on the ceiling collapse; Year 2 revolt coin in the street.
  • Carved stone block with a seven-branched menorah flanked by amphorae and columns.
  • Frescoed walls and columns; mosaic begun but cut off at both ends.
  • The quarter was abandoned and robbed after the Roman conquest; alluvium covered it; no reoccupation until the 1970s.
  • No dedicatory inscription naming the building or its builders is reported in the preliminary report.

Open questions

  • Function of the carved stone: the excavators (HA-ESI 125, 2013) read it as representing the Second Temple and used as a prayer table; the 2024-25 final volume (Talgam, Avshalom-Gorni, Najar, From the Magdala Stone to the Syriac Bema) treats it at length in relation to later synagogue and church liturgical furniture. Alternative readings have not been reviewed for this entry.
  • When it became a synagogue: the excavators assign synagogue use to the two later phases of a building first built in the mid-1st century BCE; the 43 CE coin under the mosaic dates only the latest floor. Whether the first synagogue phase predates Jesus' ministry (c. 28-30 CE) is not settled by the preliminary report's evidence.
  • 'Earliest in the Galilee': the excavators state the synagogue 'is the earliest one discovered so far in the Galilee'; this is a claim about present evidence, dependent on the dating of other early synagogues.

The Find

  • DateEarly Roman: a building of the mid-1st century BCE (early phase, probably not yet a synagogue), used as a synagogue in two later phases; abandoned at the time of the Great Revolt (66-70 CE)
  • How datedExcavators' stratigraphy (HA-ESI 125, 2013): Stratum 3 pottery 50 CE to end of 1st century CE; glass early 1st century to 40 CE; Hasmonean and 1st-century CE coins, including a coin of 43 CE in the foundation of the reading-room mosaic, a Year 2 Great Revolt coin in the street, and a coin of 80 CE on the synagogue's ceiling collapse.
  • Discovered2009 (salvage excavation seasons 2009-2013), Israel Antiquities Authority, directed by Dina Avshalom-Gorni and Arfan Najar, before construction of a hotel, Northern part of the Migdal antiquities site, NW shore of the Sea of Galilee ('Hawaii Beach'), at the foot of Mount Arbel, c. 4 km north of Tiberias
  • Now heldIn situ at Migdal (Magdala). The whereabouts of the original carved stone vs. a replica were Not confirmed in a published museum record
  • MaterialRoughly hewn basalt walls; basalt column fragments; mosaic and painted plaster (fresco); carved limestone block

Biblical Passages

  • Matt 4:23; Mark 1:39; Luke 4:44Jesus went 'throughout all Galilee, teaching in their synagogues'. Magdala is a Galilean lakeshore town with a synagogue in use in the 1st century CE; no text places Jesus in this building.
  • Luke 8:2; Mark 15:40; John 20:1-18'Mary called Magdalene' - named after Magdala (Migdal). The excavation shows the Jewish town of that name in the Early Roman period.
  • Luke 4:16-20A synagogue with a scroll read on the Sabbath; the Magdala reading room with benches around a central space and a carved stone table is the physical setting of such a reading, per the excavators' interpretation.
